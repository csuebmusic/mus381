Module 03 · Recording, sample prep & library building

the mixer

In Lectures 1 and 2 the destination of every signal flow was an audio interface: a small box with one or two inputs, plugged into the lab Mac, getting one signal at a time into the digital audio workstation, or DAW (the recording software running on the computer). On this page, sending a signal "to the DAW" means sending it through the audio interface into that software. One interface works when there's one mic and one sound to record. It stops working when there are seven mics on stage and the engineer needs to balance, EQ, and route each one to several destinations at once. That's the job of a mixer. A mixer doesn't replace the audio interface; in a studio setup the mixer goes between the mics and the interface. The mics plug into the mixer, the engineer shapes and combines the signals on the console, and the mixer's outputs feed the audio interface, which still gets the result into the DAW. The interface becomes the link between the analog console and the digital recording.

That description fits an analog mixer, the kind in our studio. Every signal path on an analog mixer is a real voltage moving through physical circuitry. The mixer works only with analog voltages, and getting its output into a computer takes a separate audio interface. A digital mixer is a different piece of equipment: its mixing, EQ, routing, and effects all happen as math on digital audio samples. Many digital mixers (Behringer X32, Allen & Heath SQ, Soundcraft Si, Yamaha QL, and others) put the analog-to-digital conversion inside the same box as the routing and connect directly to a computer over USB or Ethernet. In that setup the digital mixer is the audio interface, and every channel on the console can be a recording input to the DAW. The routing concepts are the same on both kinds (channel strips, aux sends, subgroups, masters, inserts), and what you learn on our analog Toft applies to any digital console you'll work on. The two kinds differ in feel and in how settings get saved.

1 · the mixer as the place where signals combine

A mixer (also called a mixing console, or just a console; engineers also say board or desk) is the piece of equipment in the middle of every multi-source audio setup. Each source plugs into its own channel, and each channel has its own controls (level, EQ, routing, sometimes more). The channels combine into one or more outputs that feed speakers, headphones, or a DAW.

The mixer's job is the same in every context: take many signals in, let the engineer shape each one, and send the combined result out. Live sound and studio recording use the same hardware. What changes is what's connected at the inputs and where the outputs go.

Our studio's console is the Toft Audio Designs Series ATB, designed by Malcolm Toft. Toft was an engineer at London's Trident Studios and co-founded Trident Audio Developments, the studio's console-building offshoot, in the early 1970s. Trident consoles were built for studios that recorded David Bowie, Queen, Stevie Wonder, and many others. The Toft ATB is a smaller, lower-cost design for project studios, with the same channel-strip layout as a console in a commercial studio.

The console has three kinds of strips, arranged left to right:

  1. The sixteen input strips take in signals from mics, instruments, and the DAW, one channel per strip.
  2. The eight submaster strips combine many channels into group outputs for the DAW, and they also handle DAW returns.
  3. The single master strip has the console's final stereo output, the controls for what the engineer hears in the room, and talkback.

2 · the channel strip

Every input on the console is a vertical strip with the same set of controls, top to bottom. Learn one strip and you know all of them.

At the top of the strip is the input section. The +48V button turns phantom power on (you met it in Lecture 1). The I/P REV (input reverse) button swaps two signal paths inside the channel; section 3 covers it. Pressing LINE switches the channel from the mic input on the back to the line input on the back. A red knob labeled INPUT GAIN sets how much amplification the preamp applies. It's the same kind of control as the interface gain knob you set in Lab 1 for peaks of -12 to -6 dBFS in Audacity; here it sets the level going into the channel.

The strip also has two small switches for phase reverse and an 80 Hz high-pass filter. Phase reverse flips the signal's polarity, turning the waveform upside down (sometimes needed when two mics on the same source are partly canceling each other). The 80 Hz filter rolls off everything below 80 Hz, useful for cutting room rumble or wind on vocal mics.

Phase, again

You met phase in Lecture 1, inside the XLR cable: two wires carrying the same audio, one flipped upside down, summed at the interface, where external noise cancels (each peak of the noise adds to a matching trough, and the sum is silence). The phase-reverse button on the channel strip does the same flip, here on the whole signal. Two mics on the same source can produce signals that are partly out of phase with each other: some frequencies cancel, and the sound turns thin and hollow. Press phase reverse on one of the two channels to flip that channel's polarity, and listen for whether the sum gets stronger or weaker. Stronger means the two mics had been canceling each other, and the flip fixed it. Weaker means leave the button off.

The four-band equalizer (EQ) has four pairs of knobs: high, high-mid, low-mid, and low. Each pair is a frequency control (which band of the spectrum to affect) and a boost/cut control (how much to change it). Each band can boost or cut by 15 dB. The high and low bands are shelving (everything above or below a frequency moves together); the two middle bands are peaking (a bell curve centered on the chosen frequency). One button switches the whole EQ section into or out of the signal path.

Same operation, different surface

The EQ you used in Audacity (Effect → EQ and Filters → Filter Curve EQ) and the EQ on this channel strip both boost or cut specific frequency ranges of the signal. The surface is different. In Audacity, you click points on a curve and the software shapes the audio mathematically; on the console, you turn knobs and an analog circuit shapes the signal as the voltage flows through it.

The other difference is what each EQ changes. Audacity's Filter Curve EQ changes the audio data on the track when you click Apply (destructive: you can undo it during the session, but once you save and close, the change is permanent). The console's EQ shapes the signal as it passes through the channel strip on its way to the output. Whatever's plugged into the input (the mic on stage, the DAW playback at mixdown) keeps making the sound it was making; only the signal coming out of the channel is shaped. Turn the EQ knobs back to flat, or press the EQ In/Out button, and the channel passes the signal through unchanged. This is non-destructive: you can change your mind freely, take after take, and the source stays as it was.

The strip has six aux sends: six green knobs, one per aux. Each one taps the signal at this channel and sends a copy to a separate output on the back of the console (the AUX MASTERS jacks). Each aux knob sets the level of its own tap. Aux 1 is always pre-fader: the tap comes before the fader, and the fader doesn't affect the aux level. Auxes 2 through 6 switch between pre-fader and post-fader with a PRE button. Post-fader means the tap comes after the fader, and the fader controls the aux level along with the main channel signal. Lab 3 on Wednesday covers what aux sends are used for.

A river with taps

Think of the channel's signal as a river flowing from the input, through the strip, to the fader and out into the master mix. On the console the river runs from the top of the strip down; the diagram below lays it out left to right. The aux sends are taps along the river: small pipes that draw off a copy of the water and run it somewhere else, without slowing the main river. Each aux knob sets how much water its pipe takes; turn it down to zero and that pipe sends nothing.

The taps don't change the main signal. Turning an aux knob up or down leaves the signal reaching the master mix as it was. The aux taps are parallel: they pull a copy off the main signal path without subtracting from it. One channel can feed many destinations at once. The vocal mic feeds the master mix (for the audience), and the singer's stage monitor (through Aux 1), and a reverb unit (through Aux 4), all at the same time, each at its own level.

The monitor section has a second, smaller level control, labeled MON LEVEL, with its own MON PAN. It's a second input on the same strip, with its own route into the master mix, and it's what makes this console in-line. Section 3 covers the monitor section.

Near the bottom are two small buttons. SOLO isolates this channel in the control-room speakers (the same solo you used in Audacity, in hardware). MUTE silences the channel without changing the fader position.

The red channel pan knob places this channel left or right in the stereo image.

Beside the fader are the routing buttons. L-R assigns this channel directly to the master mix; 1-2, 3-4, 5-6, and 7-8 assign it to one of the four subgroup pairs (subgroups 1 through 8). The buttons are independent, and any combination can be engaged. The usual setup is one or the other: L-R for a channel that goes straight to the master mix, or a subgroup pair for a channel you're grouping with others first. The pan knob sets the left-right split across whichever pair the signal reaches.

How the pan knob splits the signal across a subgroup pair

Say you have a mic plugged into channel 1, and you press the 1-2 button on that channel. The channel's signal is now routed to subgroups 1 and 2. Subgroup 1 is the left side of the pair and subgroup 2 the right (odd-numbered subgroups on the left, even on the right). What happens next depends on the channel's pan knob.

With the pan knob in the center, 50% of the signal goes to subgroup 1 and 50% to subgroup 2. Pan all the way left: 100% to subgroup 1, 0% to subgroup 2. Pan all the way right: 100% to subgroup 2, 0% to subgroup 1. Anywhere in between is a smooth ratio (75/25, 60/40, and so on).

Each subgroup is a bus: a point inside the console where many channel signals combine into one. The technical name for that combining is mixing, also called summing (the more literal word: the bus adds the channel signals together). Two subgroups used as a left-right pair, with the channel pan knob splitting the level between them, form a stereo bus. The master mix works the same way at a larger scale, summing every channel routed to L-R.

The fader is the main level control for this channel. It sets the channel's level in the master mix, or in the subgroup pair it's routed to. The scale beside it runs from minus infinity at the bottom (fader all the way down, no audio passing through) up past -40, -30, -20, -10, and -5, through 0 (a thicker line, usually marked with a small notch), to +5 and +10 at the top. The 0 mark is unity gain, or just unity: the fader passes the signal through at the same level it came in at. Above unity, the fader boosts the signal (up to +10 dB on this console). Below unity, it cuts: -10 dB is a noticeable drop, -20 dB a sharp one, and further down the signal is barely audible. These numbers are decibels of change relative to unity. They aren't dBFS readings like the ones on Audacity's meter. The marks are spread out near the top of the scale and bunched together toward the bottom. Around unity, a small move changes the level by a dB or two; near the bottom, the same move changes it by many dB.

the back of the console

Each strip's input jacks and some of its output jacks are on the back of the console.

From the top down, in the order they appear on the panel:

  • LINE is a 1/4-inch TRS jack, the channel's balanced line-level input. A synth or another line-level source plugs in here.
  • MON is also a 1/4-inch TRS balanced line-level input. It feeds the channel's monitor section (the pink MON LEVEL knob on the front of the strip): turn that knob up, and the signal coming in here reaches the master mix.
  • INSERT is a 1/4-inch TRS jack wired as send and return on one cable. It's the channel's insert point, where you patch an outboard processor (a compressor or an external EQ, for example) into the channel's signal path.
    A third way to use a TRS cable

    In Lecture 2 you met TRS as a cable with three conductors (tip, ring, sleeve) and saw two ways to wire it: balanced (tip = signal, ring = inverted signal, sleeve = ground) and unbalanced stereo (tip = left, ring = right, sleeve = ground).

    On an insert, tip = send (the channel's signal going out to the outboard processor), ring = return (the processor's output coming back into the channel), and sleeve = ground. Here the two signal conductors form a third arrangement, a two-way loop: one carries the signal out and the other carries the processed signal back, on the same physical cable. The signal leaves the channel through the tip, goes through whatever processor you've patched in, returns through the ring, and re-enters the channel further down the strip. This is what engineers mean by "insert send and return on a single TRS."

    Cables wired this way are sometimes called insert cables: a TRS plug at one end, two TS plugs at the other (one labeled "send," one labeled "return"), for patching the loop into a piece of gear with separate input and output jacks.

    Insert send vs. aux send

    Both an insert and an aux send have a connection called "send" leaving the strip, and the word means something different in each case.

    An aux send is a parallel branch. The aux pulls a copy of the channel's signal off at a tap point and routes it somewhere else. The main signal through the channel keeps going at full strength. The result is two signals at once: the original on the channel and a copy at the aux destination. Nothing comes back to the channel from an aux send. The copy can come back to the master section through its destination, though: a reverb return blended into the master mix, for example. The river-with-taps diagram earlier in this section shows this branch.

    An insert is a serial loop. The insert breaks the channel's signal path: the signal leaves the strip at the tip, runs through the outboard processor, and the processed signal comes back through the ring to take its place. While the loop is active, the original unprocessed signal is no longer on the strip. The round trip happens in real time, with no audible delay between leaving the strip and coming back.

    A rule of thumb for which effects go on an insert and which on an aux send: if the effect replaces the signal, use an insert; if the effect adds something alongside the signal, use an aux send. Compressors, limiters, gates, de-essers, external EQs, and distortion all work on the signal itself, and they go on inserts. Reverb, delay, and the modulation effects (chorus, flanger, phaser) produce a separate wet signal that plays alongside the dry one, and they go on aux sends. Advanced setups have exceptions (parallel compression puts a compressor on an aux send to blend it with the dry signal; "insert reverb" exists for special cases), but the rule covers most of the decisions you'll make.

  • DIR. O/P is a 1/4-inch jack, the channel's direct output: a tap of this channel's signal that goes straight to whatever you plug in here, independent of the channel's routing buttons. On the Toft ATB the tap is post-fader (taken after the channel fader), and the fader position sets the direct-out level too.
  • MIC is a female XLR socket (the large round connector at the bottom of each channel), the channel's balanced mic-level input. The dynamic mic you used in Lab 1 plugs in here, and so does the mic-level output of a DI box.

3 · in-line architecture · one strip, two inputs

The Toft ATB is an in-line console: every input strip has two inputs, and both can be active at once. The main input passes through the big fader (section 2): mic or line on the back, then INPUT GAIN, EQ, the fader, and the routing buttons, out to L-R or a subgroup pair. The second, smaller input passes through the monitor section in the middle of the strip. It's a separate signal at the MON jack on the back, with its own level (MON LEVEL) and its own pan (MON PAN), routed to the master mix.

"monitor" on this console is a confusing word

The label MON on the back of the console and MON LEVEL on the front both use the word monitor. Here the word names the source, not the studio speakers. In the era of multitrack tape, "monitoring" a recorded track meant hearing its playback come back into the console alongside whatever was being newly recorded. Engineers used it to check each new take against the earlier layers. The MON jack on the back is that playback-return input.

the I/P REV button

At the top of every input strip is the I/P REV (input reverse) button. Press it, and the two inputs on that strip swap. Whatever was coming in at the MIC or LINE jack now passes through the monitor section; whatever was coming in at the MON jack now passes through the channel path, with full EQ, aux sends, and the big fader. A common use is at mixdown, when an engineer wants the full channel path on a recorded track that came back through the smaller monitor section during tracking.

4 · the submaster section

To the right of the sixteen input strips is the submaster section: eight narrower strips that handle group outputs, monitor returns, and effects returns.

The eight submaster strips fill the left side of the photo. The two rows of controls along the very top, the L/R VU meters and the six green Aux Masters knobs, belong to the master section (section 5).

Each submaster strip has four labeled parts, top to bottom: a stereo effects return, a bargraph meter, a tape return, and the subgroup fader at the bottom.

stereo FX return (top of strip)

The stereo effects return is a self-contained stereo input for a signal coming back from an outboard effects unit (a reverb or a delay, for example), plugged in at the back of the console. It has two knobs and a button, top to bottom:

  • The red square MUTE button at the very top silences this effects return without changing any of the other settings.
  • The small black BAL knob adjusts the left-right balance of the stereo return.
  • The pink FX RET level knob sets how much of the effects return joins the master mix.

bargraph meter

The bargraph has twelve LED segments, from -20 dBu at the bottom to +15 dBu at the top. dBu is a measure of analog voltage. It's a different scale from the dBFS on Audacity's meter, where 0 is the digital ceiling. The meter shows the level of either the subgroup output going to the DAW or the tape return coming back, depending on the TAPE button below it.

tape return (middle of strip)

The whole middle block of controls (PRE, AUX 5, AUX 6, MON LEVEL, MON PAN, SOLO, TAPE) is one named part of the strip. The label "Tape Return" describes what these controls do during playback, when the strip is monitoring the DAW's output coming back into the console. The same controls also work on the subgroup output going to the DAW: the TAPE button at the bottom of the block selects which signal the controls (and the bargraph above) act on.

At the top of the block are AUX 5 and AUX 6, the submaster strip's two aux sends, with a PRE button. They share buses with the channel auxes 5 and 6. Use them to send the whole subgroup to a stage monitor or a headphone cue. The PRE button sets whether the tap comes before or after MON LEVEL.

Aux buses

Each aux has its own bus: a single horizontal conductor that runs the length of the console, and every channel and submaster connects to it through its aux knob. Aux 5 is one bus. Everything that turns up its aux 5 knob adds a copy of its signal to that bus, which ends at the Aux 5 Master in the master section and leaves the console at the AUX MASTERS 5 jack on the back.

The submaster aux sends use these same aux 5 and aux 6 buses. The console has no aux 7 or aux 8.

Below the aux sends are MON LEVEL (a red knob) and MON PAN (a small black knob), the submaster's route into the master mix. MON LEVEL sets how much of the subgroup joins the master mix, and MON PAN places it left or right in the stereo image. With MON LEVEL all the way down, the subgroup goes only to the SUBMASTER OUTPUTS jack (the send to the DAW); turn it up and the subgroup also reaches the master mix. These two controls do for a subgroup what the L-R button and channel pan do for an input strip.

The submaster's MON controls feed the master mix, not the control-room speakers. The master strip's MONITOR LEVEL knob (section 5) is a different control: it sets the volume of the control-room speakers.

The white SOLO pushbutton sends just this submaster to the solo bus, muting the other submasters in the control room while you listen. It's the input strip's SOLO button, applied to a group.

The red TAPE pushbutton selects the source for the whole block. Up: the live subgroup output, summed from whichever input channels are routed here. Down: the playback coming back from the DAW through the MONITOR RETURNS jack on the back. The name TAPE is a holdover from when those returns came from a tape machine. Today it switches between "what's going to the DAW" and "what's coming back from the DAW."

subgroup fader (bottom of strip)

A 100mm fader sets the level of this subgroup's output at the SUBMASTER OUTPUT jack on the back: the level the DAW gets. It doesn't affect the subgroup's level in the master mix, which MON LEVEL sets. The fader scale matches the input strip's: minus infinity at the bottom, 0 (unity) marked with a thicker line, +10 at the top.

summing: what happens at the destination

When several channels are routed to the same subgroup pair (section 2), their signals mix together in that subgroup (the summing from section 2's bus callout). The subgroup carries the combined output of every channel routed to it, with each channel's pan knob setting the left-right split between the two subgroups in the pair.

Take a drum kit. Say four drum mics (a kick, a snare, and a pair of overhead mics for the cymbals and the whole kit) all route to subgroup pair 1-2. Kick and snare are panned center (each splits 50/50 between subgroups 1 and 2); the left overhead is panned hard left (100% to subgroup 1); the right overhead is panned hard right (100% to subgroup 2). The four channels become two combined signals on the subgroup pair, and the kit is now a single stereo group inside the console. From here the subgroup pair carries the kit as a stereo pair, subgroup 1 with the left side and subgroup 2 with the right. The controls on the two submaster strips set what happens to it on its way out.

Where the subgroup pair goes

From the subgroup, the combined signal goes to two places, each with its own control on the submaster strip:

  1. It leaves out the back of the console at the SUBMASTER OUTPUTS jacks (subgroup 1 at SUBMASTER OUTPUT 1, subgroup 2 at SUBMASTER OUTPUT 2, and so on). The subgroup fader at the bottom of the strip sets this level. These jacks usually feed eight inputs of the audio interface and the DAW.
  2. It goes into the master mix through MON LEVEL and MON PAN in the middle of the strip, then on to MASTER O/P with everything else in the master mix.

The two destinations are independent. The DAW can get one level (set by the subgroup fader) while the master mix gets another (set by MON LEVEL).

With four drum mics routed to subgroups 1-2 only (the 1-2 button engaged, L-R off), the kit reaches the DAW through the subgroup faders and reaches the master mix through the MON LEVEL knobs.

the back of the submaster section

Top to bottom, the four rows of submaster jacks are:

  • STEREO FX RETURNS is the top row: eight stereo inputs on sixteen physical jacks, for outboard effects units such as reverbs and delays. Each pair feeds the MUTE, BAL, and FX RET controls at the top of one submaster strip on the front.
  • MONITOR RETURNS is a row of eight jacks that bring eight tracks of DAW playback back into the console. Each one is wired in parallel to two places inside the console: the matching submaster strip's tape-return controls (when its TAPE button is down) and the LINE jack of the matching input channel, 1 through 8. The two jacks aren't independent inputs; they're two physical sockets on the same internal input, and only one of them should have a cable in it at a time. At mixdown, a DAW already plugged into MONITOR RETURNS 1-8 during tracking can be brought into the channel strips, with full EQ, aux sends, and L-R assign, by pressing LINE on each channel, without re-patching at the back.
  • SUBGROUP INSERT is a row of eight jacks, one insert send/return per subgroup, for patching external processing (a compressor, an EQ, a tape simulator) across the whole subgroup before the subgroup fader. It's the subgroup version of the channel insert from section 2: the same TRS wiring (send on the tip, return on the ring), on a summed signal instead of a single channel.
  • SUBMASTER OUTPUTS is a row of eight jacks where the eight subgroup outputs leave the console. The subgroup fader on the front sets the level here. These outputs usually feed eight inputs on the audio interface, and each subgroup records to the DAW as its own track.

The bottom row of jacks (AUX MASTERS) and the left side of the photo (master outputs, master inserts, two-track returns, speaker outputs) belong to the master section (section 5).

5 · the master section

The master strip is the single strip on the far right of the console (the right third of the group/master photo):

The master section handles everything that affects the whole console rather than any single channel. Two rows of its controls are along the very top of the section, above the eight submaster strips from section 4; the master strip itself fills the column on the far right.

The two round needle meters at the very top, labeled LEFT and RIGHT, are the L/R VU meters. VU meters respond more slowly than peak meters: they average the signal over a short window, and they show perceived loudness better than instantaneous peaks. The needle moves with the music in a way the bargraph segments don't.

The six green Aux Masters knobs run in a row across the top of the section, one per aux. Every aux send on the console (the six on each input channel, plus auxes 5 and 6 on each submaster strip) feeds one of these six masters. The Aux Master knob sets the overall level of that aux's output before it leaves the back of the console at the matching AUX MASTERS jack. Each Aux Master works like a master fader for one of the six parallel mixes the aux sends create.

The remaining controls run down the master strip on the far right of the section, top to bottom.

The round HEAD PHONE jack near the top of the strip is a stereo output for the engineer's headphones. PHONES LEVEL, a few controls down, sets its level.

Three pushbuttons, 2TK DIG RET (from the optional digital card), 2TK 1 RET, and 2TK 2 RET (analog), switch the control-room speakers from the master mix to one of the two-track return inputs. Press one to A/B the mix you've recorded against the master mix coming out of the console.

What "A/B-ing a mix" means

A/B is a comparison: you have two versions of the same audio, you switch between them (A, then B, then A again), and you listen for what changes. The two versions could be the master mix and a recording of that mix, two different masters of the same song, your mix and a commercial reference track, or a mix before and after some processing.

The 2-track return buttons are the A/B switch built into the console. The master mix is "A"; press a 2TK RET button and you hear "B" (whatever is plugged into that return). Release the button and you're back at A. Press a different 2TK RET button and you're at C, the next thing under comparison.

The ear has a short memory. It's hard to tell whether a mix sounds better or worse unless you can switch back and forth quickly, and the button lets you compare the two in seconds, while the impressions are still fresh.

The pink SOLO MASTER knob sets the level of the solo bus in the control room. When any channel or submaster has its SOLO button engaged, this knob sets how loud the soloed signal plays in the control-room speakers.

The pink PHONES LEVEL knob sets the headphone jack's level. It's a separate knob from MON LEVEL, a few controls down.

The master bargraph meters are a 12-segment L/R pair showing the console's stereo output. Like the submaster bargraphs, they read analog level, not dBFS. The course uses them as its level reference on the console. They respond faster than the VU meters at the top of the section and catch peaks the VU needles smooth over.

two outputs from the master section: MASTER O/P and the speakers

The master section has two outputs going to two different places. Both carry the master mix, but they split after the master fader, and the controls on either side don't affect the other.

The first output is MASTER O/P (L and R, balanced jacks on the back): the master mix on its way out of the console. It goes to the audio interface and the DAW, or in a live show to the front-of-house (FOH) amplifiers driving the audience speakers. The master fader at the bottom of the strip sets the level out of MASTER O/P.

The second output is the speaker output, which leaves at either the MAIN SPKR jacks (L and R, the main pair of control-room speakers) or the ALT SPKR jacks (L and R, the second pair). It's what the engineer hears in the room while working. Three controls, ALT MONITOR, MONO, and MONITOR LEVEL, shape this speaker output; they don't affect MASTER O/P.

After the split, the engineer can adjust the speaker side freely (turn the room down, listen in mono, switch to the alt pair) without changing what the DAW is capturing. That's the usual wiring. MB2508 is wired differently: the control-room speakers are on MASTER O/P, and the speaker output at MAIN SPKR feeds only a subwoofer. The Lab 4 studio handout has MB2508's speaker wiring.

The white ALT MONITOR button switches the speaker output from the MAIN SPKR jacks to the ALT SPKR jacks at the back of the console. With two pairs of control-room speakers, you can A/B between them ("does the mix translate to the smaller pair as well as the big pair?").

Two pairs of control-room speakers

A mix that sounds great on one set of speakers can sound thin or muddy on another. Mix engineers call how well a mix holds up across different speakers translation. A mix that translates well sounds intentional everywhere: on big studio speakers, in a car, on a phone, on earbuds. A mix that translates poorly sounds right in one place and broken everywhere else.

Engineers check translation by switching between two pairs of speakers during the mix. A common setup is one main pair (large, full-range, accurate, the reference) and one alternate pair (smaller, more consumer-grade, closer to what most listeners own): Yamaha NS-10s, Auratones, Avantone MixCubes, even a small Bluetooth speaker in the corner. The engineer mixes mostly on the main pair, presses ALT MONITOR to A/B against the alternate, fixes whatever sounds wrong on the smaller pair, and switches back.

Both speaker outputs come from the same master section. The alternate pair plays the same mix through a different physical filter (smaller drivers, less low end, different room interaction), with nothing to unplug.

The white MONO button combines L and R into a single mono signal in the control-room speakers. The mix itself stays stereo at MASTER O/P. MONO lets you hear the mix collapsed to one channel, the way mono radio, a single phone speaker, or a single-speaker venue plays it.

The red MONITOR LEVEL knob is the master volume for the control-room speakers (whichever pair ALT MONITOR selects). It doesn't affect MASTER O/P.

talkback and master fader

The talkback system is a small microphone built into the console (the round grille labeled T.B. MIC), a level knob above it (T.B. LEVEL), and two red pushbuttons below it, TALK TO AUXES and TALK TO GROUPS. The engineer in the control room uses them to talk to the musicians in the live room and to put a slate on a take.

Talkback in practice: two buttons, two jobs

The two talkback buttons look alike but do very different things, and pressing the wrong one shows up at a bad moment (during playback of a take you thought was clean).

TALK TO AUXES sends the engineer's voice to the aux outputs, which usually feed the performers' headphones in the live room. The musicians hear the engineer ("hold on, I think we lost the click track"), and nothing gets recorded. This is the everyday button: use it whenever you want to say something to the band between takes, or during a take if you have to.

TALK TO GROUPS sends the engineer's voice to the subgroups and the master mix, and it gets recorded onto whatever tracks are armed. This is for slating the take: at the top of every take, the engineer announces the take number ("Song one, take three"), and when you're sorting through forty takes later, every one is labeled at the head of the audio file. The slate becomes part of the recording. Hit record, press TALK TO GROUPS, announce the slate, and release.

The film-set equivalent is the clapper board (the slate that snaps shut at the start of every shot). In audio recording, the engineer's voice and this button take its place.

The master fader is the large blue 100mm stereo fader at the bottom of the strip. It sets the level of the master mix on its way out at MASTER O/P (the DAW side) and on its way to the speaker output (the room side).

6 · the console as a creative space

The console's layout is a set of design choices: how many channels, how many aux sends, how many subgroups, where the EQ goes in the chain, what the I/P REV button swaps, whether the submaster MON LEVEL feeds the master mix or something else. Each choice opens some patching possibilities and closes off others. A console works well when its flexibility matches the work an engineer wants to do.

That work is partly following recipes and partly invention. The recipes are common patches: pre-fader for stage monitors, post-fader for reverb sends, the drum kit to a subgroup pair, the vocal on Aux 1. The rest is working out what to do when the recipes don't fit, or when the session calls for something the usual patching doesn't cover: a parallel compression chain that needs an aux return blended with the dry signal, a delay that feeds a reverb on a different aux, a click track that has to reach two performers but not a third. Solving those problems means designing new signal paths through the console, and that design is part of the creative work of a recording.

The puzzle is small at first (where does this mic go, how do I get this track to the performer's headphones) and grows with practice. Engineers who've worked on the same console for years still find new patches in it. Engineers moving between consoles use the same routing logic: channel, subgroup, aux, and master work the same way even when the specific jacks and switches differ. What you're learning here is the routing vocabulary. The Toft is one place to learn it, the way one piano is one place to learn the keyboard.

The question to keep in mind as you route signals in Wednesday's lab, and again on your next console: what does this signal need to do, and what's the cleanest path through the console that gets it there? Sometimes there's one obvious answer. More often there are several, each with its own trade-off, and choosing among them is part of the craft.

vocabulary

  • An analog mixer is a console where the audio signals are real voltages moving through physical circuitry. Our studio's Toft ATB is one. Getting its output into a computer takes a separate audio interface.
  • A digital mixer is a console where the audio signals are samples processed as math. It usually has analog-to-digital conversion inside the same box as the routing, and the mixer is also the audio interface, connecting directly to a computer.
  • A channel is one input on the mixer, with its own set of controls. A console with sixteen channels has sixteen of these.
  • A channel strip is the vertical column of controls for one channel.
  • An in-line console is a console where each input strip has two inputs: a main input on the big channel fader and a second, smaller input through the strip's monitor section. Both can be active at once, and a single strip can send a live mic to the DAW while it brings a recorded track back from the DAW.
  • A bus is a summing point inside the console where many signals combine into one. The subgroup buses, the aux buses, and the master mix are all buses.
  • Mix and sum both name the combining operation on a bus. Many channels reach the bus, each at the level its fader and routing set, and the bus adds them together into one signal. "Mix" is the everyday word; "sum" is the more literal technical term.
  • The master mix is the main stereo mix the console builds from all its inputs. Every input channel's L-R button, every input strip's monitor section, every submaster's MON LEVEL and MON PAN, and every FX RET feed it. The master fader controls it, and it leaves the console at MASTER O/P.
  • MASTER O/P is the pair of balanced 1/4-inch jacks on the back of the console where the master mix leaves. It's cabled to the audio interface and the DAW, or to the front-of-house (FOH) amplifiers driving the audience speakers in a live show. In MB2508 it also feeds the control-room speakers.
  • The speaker output is a separate path out of the master section to the control-room speakers, leaving at MAIN SPKR (or ALT SPKR when ALT MONITOR is engaged). The three monitor controls on the master strip (MONO, MONITOR LEVEL, ALT MONITOR) shape it, and they don't affect MASTER O/P. In MB2508 the speaker output feeds only the subwoofer.
  • The control-room speakers are the speakers in the room where the engineer works. They're usually fed by the speaker output; in MB2508 they're on MASTER O/P.
  • A subgroup is a bus that groups several channels into a sub-mix before sending it on to a destination. On the Toft, each subgroup has its own fader (its level out to the DAW) and its own route into the master mix (MON LEVEL). The Toft has eight subgroups, controlled by the eight submaster strips.
  • A submaster, on this console, is the strip that controls a subgroup. Both names are in use; "submaster" is the Toft's label.
  • An aux send is a separate tap from each channel to a separate destination. With aux sends, the engineer builds a different mix of the same channels for a different purpose (a performer's headphone cue, a reverb send, a stage monitor).
  • Pre-fader and post-fader name where an aux taps the channel. A pre-fader tap comes before the channel fader, and the fader doesn't affect the aux level. A post-fader tap comes after the channel fader, and the fader does affect it. Headphone cues are usually pre-fader; reverb sends are usually post-fader.
  • Unity gain is the position on a fader or knob where the signal passes through at the same level it came in at, with no boost or cut. It's marked 0 dB on the scale, usually with a thicker line or a notch. Above unity boosts the signal; below unity reduces it.
  • A direct out is a per-channel output that taps the channel's post-fader signal and sends it straight to a single destination (usually one track of a DAW), independent of the routing buttons.
  • An insert is a break in a channel's signal path, exposed as a send/return jack on the back of the console, where you patch in an outboard processor such as a compressor or an EQ.
  • Talkback is a small microphone in the master section, plus buttons that send the engineer's voice to the aux outputs (to performers' headphones) or onto the recording (for slating takes).
  • The word monitor has three uses on this console. (1) The MON jack on the back of an input strip, with the MON LEVEL knob in the strip's middle, is a second input on the strip that feeds the master mix, mainly for DAW playback during tracking. (2) MON LEVEL and MON PAN on a submaster strip are the subgroup's route into the master mix. (3) MONITOR LEVEL on the master strip is the volume knob for the control-room speakers. All three are labeled MON, after what engineers traditionally called the "monitoring" side of the recording process. A performer's stage monitor in live sound is a different use of the word (Lab 3 covers it).
  • Front-of-house (FOH) is the master mix sent to the speakers facing the audience in a live show. The "front of the house" is the audience side of a venue.
  • A headphone cue (or just cue) is a performer's headphone mix during tracking, usually built from one or more aux sends.
