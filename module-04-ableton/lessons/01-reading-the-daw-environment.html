Into the DAW

A map of the next four weeks in Ableton Live, and the ideas that carry into any DAW.

You spent two modules in Audacity, first shaping sound that was handed to you, then recording your own. Now you move into Ableton Live, the program the rest of the course runs on. Live is a full DAW, different enough from Audacity that the first thing to do is get its shape: what a DAW is, where it came from, and what changes once you are working inside one. This page is the map of the next four weeks, and the ideas on it carry past Ableton into any DAW you open later.

What a DAW is

Live is a DAW, a digital audio workstation: software for recording, editing, arranging, and mixing sound. You have already used one. Audacity is a DAW, a small one, built mostly for recording and editing a few tracks at a time. Live is the full version of the same idea.

A full DAW does more in one place. It runs many tracks at once. It holds instruments you can play and trigger. It mixes everything through a built-in console laid out like the Toft desk you sat at in Module 3, with faders, sends, and a master. Recording, editing, instruments, and mixing live together in a single window, and that is what makes Live the place where the two halves of this course, the sounds you edited and the sounds you recorded, finally come together.

A short history

The DAW is the newest tool in a long line for capturing sound and reshaping it after the fact. Recording itself is over a century old, but for most of that time a recording was fixed: once it was made, changing it was hard. The story of the DAW is the story of recordings becoming editable.

That story runs through magnetic tape. From the 1940s, studios recorded sound onto tape, and for the first time a recording could be cut apart and rearranged. Composers spliced tape with a razor blade, joining pieces of different recordings into something new. You heard the result in the Module 2 listening: musique concrète was made this way, by hand, one cut at a time.

In the 1950s the guitarist Les Paul helped develop multitrack tape, machines that held several recordings side by side so parts could be layered and balanced. A band no longer had to play all at once: a take could be built up piece by piece. Multitrack recording is still the heart of how a studio works, and it is what the tracks in Live descend from.

Tape had one hard limit. Editing it was physical, so it was permanent. A cut was a cut, with no undo, and a mistake could cost you the take. The move into digital is in large part the move past that limit.

By the late 1970s, the first systems recorded sound as digital data, as numbers a computer could store. Once sound was numbers, it could be drawn on a screen as a waveform, edited with a cursor, and copied without wearing out. The work that took a razor blade became a selection and a keystroke.

Computers also learned the other half of music: the performance. In 1983 the MIDI standard gave instruments and computers a shared language for notes, so a computer could capture which keys you played and send them back out to a synthesizer. The programs that did this were called sequencers, and they grew into the software we use now.

The two halves came together. In 1989 and 1991, Digidesign's Sound Tools and then Pro Tools recorded many tracks straight to a computer's hard disk, and through the 1990s the studio that had been a room full of tape machines became software. In 1996, Steinberg's VST let effects and instruments themselves run inside the program, using the computer's own power to shape sound as it played. A laptop could now hold the whole studio: the recorder, the mixer, the effects, and the instruments.

Ableton Live arrived in 2001, made by a small company in Berlin for musicians who wanted to perform with a computer on stage, not only record in a studio. That origin is still visible in Live today. It is why the program is called Live, why it has a grid for launching loops on the fly (the Session View), and why it can stretch a recording to match a tempo on the spot (warping). You meet both over the next four weeks.

The tools changed at every step, from blade to mouse, from tape to disk, from hardware to software. The work stayed the same: take sound, cut it, layer it, and shape it into a piece.

What changes once you are inside a DAW

Moving into Live changes editing in two ways and adds one kind of material you have not worked with before.

Nondestructive editing

In Audacity, an edit changed the file. Cut a second of sound and that second was gone from the recording on disk. That is why you kept careful copies: a wrong move was permanent.

Live works the other way. When you cut, trim, fade, or move sound in Live, you are not editing the file. You are editing a clip. A clip is a reference to an audio file: it points at the file on disk and carries a set of instructions for how to play it, where to start, where to stop, how loud, where to fade. The file itself stays whole. Change the instructions and the file underneath does not move.

This is nondestructive editing, and it is the one habit to unlearn from Module 2. Because the file never changes, you cannot ruin your source, and you can try anything. An edit you dislike is undone by changing the instruction back, not by digging out a backup. Beginners tend to edit timidly, afraid of breaking something. In Live there is nothing to break.

Live processing

The same idea runs through effects. In Audacity, applying an effect like a fade or an equalizer rewrote the samples in the file. In Live, an effect sits on the track and does its work while the music plays. Press play and Live carries out the effect in real time, building the processed sound on the spot. This is live processing, and it is where the name of the program points.

Because the processing happens during playback, an effect is a setting you can change or switch off at any time, and the recording underneath never changes. You can stack an equalizer, a compressor, and a reverb on a track, adjust them while you listen, and turn any of them off to hear the track without it. Nothing is committed until you export a finished file at the end.

MIDI

Live holds two kinds of material. One you already know: audio, the recorded sound you edited in Module 2 and captured in Module 3. The other is new, and it is called MIDI.

MIDI is data, not sound. A MIDI note contains no audio. It is a small instruction that says which pitch to play, when to play it, and how hard. On its own it makes no sound at all. To hear anything, the note has to trigger an instrument, and the instrument is what produces the audio.

The difference between audio and MIDI is the difference between a recording of a performance and the instructions for one. To make audio, a person plays an instrument, the sound moves through the air, and a microphone captures it. To make sound from MIDI, the data takes the performer's place: it tells an instrument inside Live which notes to play, and the instrument generates the sound. One captures a sound that already happened. The other holds the decisions and makes the sound on demand.

Because MIDI is only the decisions, you can change them afterward with nothing lost. Move a note to another pitch, shift its timing, play it harder or softer, or swap the instrument entirely, and the part plays back the new way. A wrong note is not a ruined take. It is one note to drag.

You meet MIDI in Wk 11, where you use it to play the samples in your own library. A note triggers a sound from your collection, and the same note moved higher or lower plays that sound higher or lower. For now it is enough to know that Live keeps audio and MIDI side by side, and that MIDI is the trigger, not the sound.

The concepts travel

You are learning Live, but you are really learning how a DAW works. The names and the layout change from one program to the next; the concepts underneath do not.

Tracks, clips, nondestructive editing, a timeline, a mixer with faders and sends, an equalizer, a compressor, a reverb: every serious DAW has these, because they are properties of digital audio and of how production works, not features unique to Ableton. Learn them once and you can sit down at an unfamiliar program and find your footing by looking for the same parts under different labels.

A few of the programs you will hear about:

  • Pro Tools: the long-running standard in recording studios, film, and television.
  • Logic Pro: Apple's studio DAW, widely used for songwriting and production.
  • Cubase: one of the oldest, strong for arranging and scoring.
  • FL Studio: a favorite for beat-making and electronic production.
  • Reaper: lightweight and inexpensive, popular for recording and editing.
  • GarageBand: Apple's free starter DAW, a simpler cousin of Logic.
  • Adobe Audition: the audio editor in the Adobe suite, common for podcasts and post-production.

That last name is where Wk 13 goes. After three weeks in Live you step into Adobe Audition, a different program many of you already know from the Adobe suite, and find the same ideas waiting: sample rate, bit depth, editing, fades, equalization, compression, multitrack. Different program, different buttons, same concepts. That session is the proof that the skills you are about to build are yours to carry, not on loan from one app.

Audio standard for this module

Your library files are already 48 kHz, the rate the course has used since Module 2, so they import into a Live set with nothing to convert. The one change this module is bit depth: you export finished work at 32-bit, up from the 24-bit you worked in before. The rate that matters for a deliverable is set when you export.
