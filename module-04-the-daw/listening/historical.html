Listening: Sampling

Building new music from recorded sound: the same idea as Module 2's musique concrète, now made with turntables, samplers, and the DAW.

In Wk 11 you used Simpler and the Drum Rack to take a recorded sound, chop it, and trigger the pieces. That move, turning recorded sound into new music, has a long history, and the four pieces below are landmarks in it. It is the same idea you met in Module 2, where Pierre Schaeffer and Pierre Henry built pieces from train sounds and a creaking door using tape and razor blades. The artists here do it with turntables, samplers, and the computer. Listen with your own final project in mind.

Before you listen: a brief history

Module 2's listening ended in the tape era. Schaeffer and Henry worked by cutting magnetic tape with a blade and splicing it back together, so building music from recorded sound meant physical labor with tape, a knife, and glue. What changed after them was the tool, not the idea.

The first new tool was the turntable. By the late 1970s, DJs in the Bronx were treating two copies of a record as an instrument: cutting between them, scratching, and looping a few seconds of a drum break by hand. Grandmaster Flash's 1981 piece is a recording of exactly that, a live performance built entirely from other people's records, mixed on three turntables in real time.

Then came the sampler, a machine that records a sound into memory so you can trigger it from a pad or a key, chop it, and loop it. Through the late 1980s and 1990s, producers built whole tracks from short fragments of old records this way. DJ Shadow made an entire album, Endtroducing....., on an Akai MPC sampler using nothing but samples from vinyl, and it holds the Guinness record as the first album made completely from samples. J Dilla turned the same machine into something personal, chopping soul and funk records into new rhythms by hand, with a feel that producers still study today. This sampler is the direct ancestor of Simpler and the Drum Rack.

The computer absorbed all of it. The Avalanches built Since I Left You from thousands of vinyl samples, stitched so smoothly that the seams disappear, work that would have taken a tape studio years. By the time you were born, anyone with a laptop could chop and layer recorded sound the way these artists did with racks of gear. You are doing it now in Ableton.

As you listen, you are hearing your own toolkit at work a few decades back. Notice what each artist does with their source material: where they loop it, chop it, pitch it, and layer it. You will be asked what you hear and what you want to try yourself.

Listen to all four, then choose one more

1 · Grandmaster Flash · The Adventures of Grandmaster Flash on the Wheels of Steel (1981)

A live mix of other people's records, cut together on three turntables in real time. The turntable used as a sampler, played by hand. About 7 minutes.

2 · DJ Shadow · Midnight in a Perfect World (1996)

From Endtroducing....., an album built entirely from samples on an MPC. This track is assembled from old records, including a piano line lifted from a 1969 song. About 5 minutes.

▶ Listen on YouTube: DJ Shadow, Midnight in a Perfect World ~5 min · opens in a new tab ↗

3 · The Avalanches · Since I Left You (2000)

Thousands of vinyl samples stitched into one seamless piece. Listen for how many different sources you can pick out. About 4 minutes.

Soul and funk records chopped into new rhythms by hand. The tracks are short, a minute or two each, and run together. Listen to at least four or five.

5 · A contemporary example, your choice

Find one piece made in the last 20 years that builds music by sampling: a producer who chops and recontextualizes recorded sound the way these four did. A few directions if you are stuck: Flying Lotus, Burial, Jamie xx, Knxwledge, Kaytranada, Madlib, or a producer whose beats you already love.

Note what you chose and why: that's part of your response.

Questions

Answer all four.

  1. What do you hear? Pick one piece and describe its source material. What are the samples, where might they come from, and how recognizable are they as their original selves?
  2. How is it shaped? Pick one piece and describe how it is built over time. What holds it together as a piece: a repeating loop, a steady beat, a single mood, a clear arc?
  3. What technique stands out? Across the pieces, name one specific sampling technique you noticed (looping, chopping, pitch-shifting, layering, the turntable cut, stuttering) and describe how it is used.
  4. What will you try? Based on this listening, name one concrete thing you want to try in your final project. Be specific: not "something cool" but "chop one recorded sound into a rhythm" or "loop a two-second fragment and build over it."
