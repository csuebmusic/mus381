Module 04 · Audio, sampling & mixing in Ableton

The DAW Environment

What a full DAW is, and why editing a clip never changes the file underneath.

You spent Modules 2 and 3 in Audacity: first editing sound that was handed to you, then recording your own. This module moves you into Ableton Live, the program the rest of the course is built around. You will load the sample library you built in Module 3 and start making pieces from it. The biggest change from Audacity is that in Live your edits never alter the original sound files, so you can experiment freely. On Wednesday you open Live and get started.

Today's gear

Take from the lab's gear storage: audio interface, headphones. Plug in and run through the start-of-session steps on the Session Routines card before continuing here.

1 · A full DAW

A DAW (digital audio workstation) is software for recording, editing, arranging, and mixing audio on a computer. By that definition, Audacity is already a DAW, and you have been doing DAW work since Week 2. So why switch?

Because Ableton Live is a full DAW, and Audacity is a narrow one. In Audacity you mostly worked with one piece of sound at a time, on a short stack of tracks, with a fixed set of editing tools. Live is built to hold many tracks playing together, to play instruments you trigger from a keyboard, and to mix everything through a built-in mixing desk. A version of it runs in most studios you will walk into.

The document you work in is called a Live Set. It plays the same role Audacity's project did: it is the file that holds your work. A Set lives inside a Project folder, which keeps the Set and the sounds it uses together in one place. Your local-first, NAS-as-sync habit does not change: you pull your work down at the start of a session and upload it at the end, the same as always.

1.1 · The audio format

You have worked at 48 kHz since Module 2, and your Module 3 sample library is already there. Live runs at the same rate, so your library files load into a Set with nothing to convert. The sample rate does not change in this module.

The one thing that does change is bit depth, and only at the end. When you export a finished piece, you export at 32-bit, a step up from the 24-bit you have been working in. It is the setting you choose in the export dialog, and it gives the final file extra headroom from all the mixing Live has done.

2 · Two views: Session and Arrangement

When you open a Live Set, you can look at it two different ways. They are called the Arrangement View and the Session View, and you switch between them with the Tab key.

The Arrangement View is a timeline. Time runs left to right, tracks stack top to bottom, and you build a piece by placing sound along the line. This is the view that will feel familiar, because it works like the Audacity window you already know. It is home base for this module, and where you will do most of your editing.

The Session View is a grid. Instead of a timeline, you get a set of slots, each holding a short piece of sound you can start and stop on the fly. Producers use it to try ideas and loop sections before committing them to a timeline. You will meet it properly once you have some sound to play with, so for now it is enough to know it is there.

Both views show the same Set. Pressing Tab changes what you are looking at, not what is stored or what you hear. Think of them as two windows onto one room, not two different rooms.

3 · The clip

The basic unit of work in Live is the clip. A clip is a single piece of sound sitting on a track: one recording, one loop, one sample from your library. You build a piece by arranging clips along the timeline.

An audio clip does not contain the sound. It contains a reference to the sound: a pointer to a file on the drive, plus instructions for which part of that file to play and how to play it. The sound file it points at is called a sample, and the samples in your Module 3 library are exactly these files. The clip is the instruction sheet; the sample is the sound.

A track holds clips and plays one of them at a time. Live has two kinds of track, audio and MIDI, and this module starts with audio. MIDI arrives next week, when you start triggering sounds from a keyboard.

4 · Nondestructive editing

That small detail, that a clip points at a file rather than containing it, is the difference that changes how you work.

In Audacity, editing changed the file. A cut deleted samples. A fade rewrote them. Once you exported and closed, those edits were baked in, which is why you kept careful copies and worked a little cautiously. That approach is called destructive editing: the edit destroys the original and replaces it with the edited version.

Live works the other way. When you trim a clip, fade it, change its volume, or stretch it, those edits live on the clip, and Live applies them as the sound plays. The sample on the drive is never touched. This is called nondestructive editing: the edit shapes playback without changing the original.

The practical payoff is large: you cannot ruin your source. Trim too much, stretch a sound until it breaks, change your mind an hour later, and the original file is still sitting there untouched, ready for you to start over. Where Module 2 rewarded caution, Live rewards experiment. Try the edit. If you do not like it, undo it or drag the clip's edge back. Nothing was lost.

Coming from Audacity

The "save a copy before you cut" habit you built in Module 2 was the right instinct for destructive editing. In Live you can relax it. The edits sit on the clip, and the file they point at is safe.

On Wednesday you put this to work: you pull your library down from the NAS, drop your own sounds into a Set as clips, and arrange and edit them on the Arrangement timeline, knowing the files themselves are never at risk.

Vocabulary

DAW (digital audio workstation)
Software for recording, editing, arranging, and mixing audio. Audacity is a narrow one; Ableton Live is a full one.
Live Set
The document you work in, the file that holds your work in Live. It lives inside a Project folder.
Project folder
The folder that keeps a Live Set and the sounds it uses together in one place.
Arrangement View
The timeline view of a Set: time left to right, tracks stacked top to bottom. Home base for editing this module.
Session View
The grid view of a Set: slots holding short pieces of sound you can start and stop on the fly. Met later in the module.
Clip
A single piece of sound on a track. An audio clip is a reference to a sound file plus instructions for how to play it, not the sound itself.
Sample
A sound file on the drive that a clip points at. The prepped sounds in your Module 3 library are samples.
Track
A lane that holds clips and plays one at a time. Live has audio tracks and MIDI tracks; this module starts with audio.
Destructive editing
Editing that changes the original file, the way Audacity does. The edit replaces the original.
Nondestructive editing
Editing that shapes playback without changing the original file, the way Live does. The edits live on the clip.
