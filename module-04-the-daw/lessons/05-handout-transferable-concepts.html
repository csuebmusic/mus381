Module 04 · The DAW

Adobe Audition

You spent this term in two programs: Audacity, where an edit changed the file, and Ableton, where an edit changed a clip and left the file alone. Adobe Audition does both. It is built for shaping recorded sound rather than creating music, which is why podcasters and video editors use it and why it carries no instruments. Today you run the editing moves you already know in Audition and see how much of it carries over.

Today's gear

Take from the lab's gear storage: audio interface, headphones. Plug in and run through the start-of-session steps on the Session Routines card before continuing here.

No deliverable today

Nothing here is graded or submitted. This is a hands-on tour to connect what you know to a new tool, so experiment freely. You still run the end-of-session routine and upload your working folder at the end, the same as every session.

What we're doing today

  1. Meet Audition's two editors, and recognize them as the two working styles you already used.
  2. Start a session and bring in a few sounds from your library.
  3. Edit one sound in the Waveform Editor: select, ripple-delete, fade.
  4. Clean it: capture a noise print, then reduce the noise.
  5. Build a small multitrack mix: levels, pan, then EQ, compression, and a limiter.
  6. See one thing that is new to you: the Spectral Frequency Display.
  7. Export the mix, then run the end-of-session routine.

Two editors, two working styles you already have

Audition opens into one of two editors, and knowing which is which is the whole orientation. The Waveform Editor works on a single audio file and edits it destructively: a cut changes the file, exactly like Audacity in Modules 2 and 3. The Multitrack Editor stacks clips on a timeline and edits them nondestructively: the edits are instructions layered on the clip, and the source files stay untouched, exactly like Ableton.

Ableton adds instruments and MIDI, so we called it a DAW. Audition stays on recording, editing, and mixing audio, with no instruments of its own, so in this course we call it an advanced audio editor. Some guides online call it a DAW anyway. The label matters less than the working styles, and you already have both.

Three guides cover the same ground at different depths. Reach for whichever fits how you like to learn. Adobe's is the source of truth, the walkthrough is a current step-by-step, and the New School guide is a printable reference you can keep open beside you.

Start a session and bring in your sounds

Make a multitrack session with File → New → Multitrack Session. Set the Sample Rate to 48000, the same rate you have worked at all term, and leave Bit Depth at 32-bit float. Name the session and choose a folder inside your working folder at ~/Documents/lastname/, so the session file and its audio stay together. Audition saves the session as a .sesx file, which points at your audio rather than swallowing it.

With the NAS still mounted from your start-of-session routine, copy a handful of sounds from your library at [path]/students/lastname/sample-library/ down to your working folder. Bring them into the session with File → Import → File, or drag them onto the Files panel. The Files panel holds the media for this session, the same job the browser does in Ableton.

Edit one sound in the Waveform Editor

Double-click a sound in the Files panel. It opens in the Waveform Editor, the destructive side, so work on a copy and keep your library original safe. This is the Audacity way of editing, one file, changes that stick.

Pick the Time Selection tool (press T), then click and drag across a part you want gone, a long gap or a stray noise. Press Ripple Delete (Delete on the Mac) to remove the selection and close the gap in one move. A plain delete would leave silence behind; Ripple Delete pulls the audio together.

Smooth the start and end so they do not click. Select the first half-second, right-click, and choose Fade In. Do the same at the end with Fade Out. This is the same fade habit you built in Audacity, where a hard edit boundary pops unless you ramp it.

Time and pitch, the tape link again

In the Multitrack Editor you can stretch a clip by dragging the small white triangles in its top corners. Right-click the clip, open Stretch, and look at the Stretch Properties. Varispeed changes pitch and duration together, faster means higher and shorter, like speeding up tape. That is the coupling you met in Module 2 and saw in Audacity's Change Speed. The other modes hold pitch while they stretch, the way Ableton's warp does.

Clean it: noise print, then reduce

This is the move that makes people choose Audition, and it is the one you already ran in Module 3 when you prepped samples. You show the software a slice of pure background noise, then ask it to subtract that noise from the whole file.

In the Waveform Editor, select one to three seconds of room tone, a stretch with no sound you want to keep. Open Effects → Noise Reduction/Restoration → Noise Reduction (Process) and click Capture Noise Print. Audition now holds a fingerprint of the noise. Select the whole file with Cmd-A, set the Noise Reduction amount to around 70 to 80 percent, press Preview to hear it, then Apply.

Keep a light touch. Pushed past about 90 percent, the reduction leaves voices sounding hollow and underwater. A little background left in sounds more natural than a heavily scrubbed file. Capturing a profile first, then reducing, is exactly the two-step shape from your sample-prep pipeline.

Build a small multitrack mix

Switch back to the Multitrack Editor (Cmd-9). Drag your cleaned sounds from the Files panel onto separate tracks. Each track has a channel strip down its left side, with a volume fader, a Mute button (M), a Solo button (S), and a pan control. Set rough levels with the faders and place sounds across the stereo field with pan. This is the Ableton mixer and the Toft console from Module 3 in another shape: the track fader is the channel fader, pan is pan, and solo lets you listen to one thing at a time.

Now the effects. Select a track and open the Effects Rack, then add devices in order. A dependable starting chain is Parametric Equalizer, then Dynamics Processing for compression, then Hard Limiter: shape the tone first, even out the loud and quiet next, and catch the peaks last. These are the same EQ, compression, and limiting you met in Module 2 and used in Ableton, wearing Adobe's names. Because this is the Multitrack side, the effects are nondestructive, so you can change or remove any of them at any time.

One thing that is new to you

Open a file in the Waveform Editor and switch to the Spectral Frequency Display using the view button at the top of the editor. Instead of the loudness shape you know from a waveform, you see frequency from low to high up the side and time across the bottom, with louder sounds drawn brighter. A cough, a chair squeak, or a phone buzz shows up as its own bright shape.

Select that shape with the Marquee or Lasso tool and press Delete, or use Heal Selection to fill it with the sound around it. Neither Audacity nor Ableton gave you this. It is a reminder that every new tool brings a few moves of its own, on top of the ones that carry over.

Export the mix

The session file is not your finished audio. To get a sound file you can hand off, use File → Export → Multitrack Mixdown → Entire Session, choose WAV at 48 kHz, and save it into your working folder. This is the same project-versus-export split you have made in every module: the .sesx holds your editing decisions, the exported WAV is the thing other people can play.

End of session

  2. During the NAS-upload phase of the routine, upload your lastname/ working folder to [path]/students/lastname/, choosing Replace when prompted. Today's practice session and your exported WAV go up with it.
