Peer Listening: Sample libraries

Browse and listen to your classmates' midterm libraries, and write about a few that gave you ideas.

After Wednesday's midterm submission, everyone's sample library is gathered in one place. This assignment asks you to browse them, listen through a handful, and write briefly about three or four that gave you ideas. A library is a different thing from a finished piece: it is a set of choices about what was worth recording and keeping, a palette someone built. You are listening as a fellow builder, scouting what other people heard and how you might use it. The response is due before class on Mon Wk 10, the first day of Module 4, and we discuss the libraries then, so come prepared.

How to browse

The libraries are gathered at /public/mus-381-fall-2026/project-02-libraries/ on the class server, with one folder per classmate. Connect with FileZilla, click the / at the top of the directory tree on the right side, then open public and navigate down. You can reach the server from inside the lab only. Download what you want to hear there, and put a copy on a USB drive or your own cloud storage if you want to listen elsewhere. Start with a classmate's README.txt: it tells you how they organized things and what is in each category. Then listen through their sounds, category by category.

There are around 25 libraries, and you do not need to hear every sample of every one. Skim the whole set, then pick three or four libraries to go deeper on. The samples are short, a few seconds each, so a single library is quick to get through. Use headphones if you can.

Don't multitask while you browse. These are your classmates' first libraries, and real time went into recording and prepping them.

What to write

Pick three or four libraries that gave you ideas, for whatever reason: a sound you wish you had recorded, a category you would not have thought of, an especially clean recording, a smart piece of organization, or just a collection with a strong character. For each one, give us:

  1. Whose library (last name or folder).
  2. One specific thing you noticed. A standout sound, a category, a recording that is especially clean, a naming or organization choice, the overall range. Be specific. Not "good library" but "the kitchen category had three knife-on-board takes that each sounded usefully different."
  3. One sound you'd borrow, and what you'd do with it. Name a specific file and say how you would use it in a piece: loop it, pitch it down, layer it under something, turn it into a beat, stretch it into a drone. This is the question Module 4 opens with, so start thinking like it now.

About 50 to 80 words per library. Specific beats long and vague. You are listening as a builder, not a critic.

Tone

This is a low-stakes assignment, but the way you write about peers' work matters. Be generous and curious. If a library didn't pull at you, write about another one (there is no requirement to critique). The libraries you choose to write about are themselves a kind of feedback: the work that gives you ideas is the work you are learning from.

Saying what you would do with someone's sound is also a gift to them, since it shows a use they may not have seen. Bring those ideas to the Mon Wk 10 discussion.

Formatting
12 pt, double-spaced, 1-inch margins. Use Times New Roman, Garamond, or another standard serif. Include your name, the course (MUS 381), the assignment, and the date at the top.
Citations
Not required, since you are writing about classmates' work rather than external sources. Identify each library by the classmate's last name or folder.
A note on giving and receiving feedback

What you write about a classmate's library may end up in front of them. Specific is better than vague, and curious is better than judgmental. You are also scouting raw material for your own Module 4 work, so listen like a builder: the library that gives you the most ideas is the one teaching you the most.
