before the techniques: zoom in, find a zero crossing

Before any of the editing moves below, learn to zoom into the waveform. Most edits look approximate when you're zoomed all the way out; zoomed in, you can see the actual shape of the wave and place a cut or fade exactly where you mean to.

Zooming goes with a second idea, the zero crossing. Remember from Mon Wk 2: the y-axis on a waveform is amplitude. The horizontal line through the middle of the track is amplitude zero, where the wave is momentarily silent on its way from positive to negative or back. A zero crossing is any point where the waveform crosses that center line.

A cut in the middle of a wave makes a click pop. If you cut a clip where the amplitude is, say, +0.6, the playback hits +0.6 and then jumps instantly to wherever the next clip starts. That instant jump is heard as a click. If you cut at a zero crossing, the amplitude is zero on both sides of the cut, with no jump to make a click. Zoom in, find a zero crossing, and cut there.

snap a selection to zero crossings

Audacity has a built-in command that moves your selection's edges to the nearest zero crossings automatically. After you make a selection, press Z. The selection edges move slightly, often by just a few samples.

Menu path: Select → At Zero Crossings · Z

try it

  1. In your scratch project, import any sound from the bank to practice on.
  2. Press Cmd + F to fit the whole sound in the window.
  3. Drag-select a region somewhere in the middle of the waveform. Don't worry about exactly where the boundaries are.
  4. Press Cmd + E to zoom into your selection. Now you can see individual cycles of the wave.
  5. Look at where your selection's left and right edges fall. Are they on the center line, or partway up or down a cycle?
  6. Press Z. Watch the edges move slightly. They're now at zero crossings.
  7. Press Cmd + F to zoom back out. Delete the track before moving on.
Habit to build

Press Z before every cut, delete, or copy; it prevents most click pops. Fades (technique 4) are the other way to clean up edit boundaries, and you can use both on the same edit.

the ten techniques

Work through the techniques below in order without skipping.

For each exercise, import the sound (or sounds) into your scratch project (File → Import → Audio… or Cmd + Shift + I) so each appears as a new track. After you've applied the technique and listened, delete the track(s) before starting the next exercise (click the track name in the track header, then Tracks → Remove Tracks). Cmd + A (select all) selects every track in the project. Practice each move; no single result needs to be finished.

1 · cut

Remove a selected region. The audio on either side closes the gap. It's like deleting a phrase from the middle of a sentence.

Where in Audacity
Edit → Cut · Cmd + X
Exercise

Browse voice-and-language/ and find a sound with someone speaking a phrase or sentence (a few words long, at most). Import it. Listen once. Then select a single word in the middle of the phrase (use your ears and the waveform to find the boundaries, drag-select with the I-beam). Press Cmd + X. Listen again.

Listen for: the cut should sound like the word was never there. The phrase should still feel coherent, just shorter. If you hear a click pop at the cut boundary, that's the edit-boundary problem from Monday's reading; we'll fix it with fades in technique 4.

2 · trim

Keep a selected region and remove everything else. The opposite of cut. Useful for isolating a single sound from a longer recording.

Where in Audacity
Edit → Remove Special → Trim Audio · Cmd + T
Where Trim is

Trim is in Edit → Remove Special, not directly under Edit.

Exercise

Browse natural-environment/ and find a longer field recording (something with multiple events: birds plus traffic, water plus wind, etc.). Import it. Listen and find one specific event you like (a single bird call, one car passing, one gust of wind). Drag-select just that event. Press Cmd + T.

Listen for: everything outside your selection is gone. You've isolated one event from a busier recording. This is how musique concrète composers extract usable fragments from raw field recordings.

3 · splice and arrange

Place sounds next to each other in time. Musique concrète composers built pieces this way: they took a fragment of one recording, placed a fragment of another after it, and a third after that. The arrangement is the composition.

Where in Audacity
There's no single "splice" command. The basic recipe is to use Edit → Copy (or Edit → Cut, to remove it from where it was) on a region from one place, then Edit → Paste at the cursor position somewhere else (on the same track or a different one).

If you want the audio you cut from to not close the gap (so the original timing is preserved), use Edit → Remove Special → Cut and leave gap (Cmd + Option + X) instead of plain Cut.

Exercise

Pick three different short sounds from three different category folders (for example: one from attack-sharp/, one from found-objects/, one from voice-and-language/). Import each as its own track; these are your source tracks. Then add an empty arrangement track below them (Tracks → Add New → Stereo Track). This is where you'll assemble the splice. Copy a piece of source 1, paste it into the arrangement track at the start; copy a piece of source 2, paste it into the arrangement track after; copy a piece of source 3, paste it after that. Aim for a short phrase, maybe 5 to 10 seconds total.

Listen for: a sequence of unrelated sounds, played in the order you chose, becomes a phrase with shape. Schaeffer built his 1948 Études de bruits from fragments placed in sequence, working with discs rather than tape.

4 · fade in and fade out

A volume ramp at the boundary of an edit. Fade-in starts from silence and ramps up; fade-out is the reverse. Even very short fades (10 to 50 milliseconds) prevent click pops at edit boundaries.

Where in Audacity
Effect → Fading → Fade In
Effect → Fading → Fade Out

The Effect menu groups effects by category. Fading is one of the categories; click it to expand. The fade is applied to whatever is currently selected, and its length equals the length of your selection: a 1-second selection makes a 1-second fade, a 50-millisecond selection makes a 50-millisecond fade.

Exercise

Find a sound from sustain-long/ (a drone, a held note, a long bowed sound). Import it. Select the first ~1 second. Apply Effect → Fading → Fade In. Then select the last ~1 second. Apply Effect → Fading → Fade Out. Listen.

Listen for: the sound now eases in instead of starting abruptly, and eases out instead of ending hard. Look at the waveform. The first and last seconds taper visibly.

5 · crossfade

The overlap of a fade-out and a fade-in: one sound fades out as another fades in. The seam between two sounds becomes smooth instead of abrupt. Editors use crossfades to hide hard cuts.

Where in Audacity
Effect → Fading → Crossfade Clips (two clips side by side on the same track)

Audacity also has Effect → Fading → Crossfade Tracks for two stacked tracks (one above the other, overlapping in time). Today's exercise uses Crossfade Clips, which works on two adjacent clips inside a single track.

Exercise

Pick two sounds with similar character (for example, two from texture-continuous/: maybe rain and wind, or two different street ambiences). Import both, then drag the second clip into the first track's lane, just after the first clip (they can touch or leave a small gap). Drag-select from about half a second before the end of the first clip to about half a second into the second, so the selection spans the join. Apply Effect → Fading → Crossfade Clips.

Listen for: instead of a hard cut from sound 1 to sound 2, you hear them blend across the join. The seam disappears. You'll use this one constantly.

6 · loop

A region played repeatedly. Useful as a working aid (loop a section while you adjust an effect) or as a compositional move (a tape-loop pattern that becomes the rhythmic base of a piece).

Where in Audacity
Two paths, depending on what you want:
  • To loop playback (the loop is a listening tool, and the audio doesn't change), make a selection, then Transport → Looping → Set Loop to Selection (Shift + L), then Transport → Looping → Loop on/off (L) to toggle looping. Press spacebar to play; the selection plays in a loop until you stop.
  • To loop in the audio (the loop becomes part of the piece), copy a clip (Cmd + C), then paste it (Cmd + V) several times in succession. Each paste places another copy at the cursor, and the repeats are now part of the audio on the track.
Exercise

Find a short rhythmic-sounding sound from found-objects/ or mechanical-electronic/ (a tap, a click, a brief mechanical pulse, something under a second). Import it. Try the playback-loop first: select it, set it as the loop, turn looping on, play. Listen for a few cycles, then stop. Now try the in-audio loop: copy the clip, paste it four or five times in succession on the same track. Play the result.

Listen for: a single short sound becomes a pulse. Repetition turns a static fragment into something with rhythm and momentum. We'll come back to looping in Module 4 with Ableton Live.

7 · reverse

Play a sound backward. The recording is the same, but its envelope is flipped: the attack becomes the release, and the release becomes the attack. Reverse changes the direction of the sound through time, not its duration or its pitch.

Where in Audacity
Effect → Special → Reverse

Reverse is in the Special category of the Effect menu, alongside other one-off transforms.

Exercise

Find a sound with a clear strike-then-decay shape from attack-sharp/ (a struck object, a single piano note, a pluck, a snap). Import it. Listen once. Then select all of it (Cmd + A) and apply Effect → Special → Reverse. Listen.

Listen for: instead of a sharp hit followed by a fade, you hear the sound rush in and end abruptly. The envelope has flipped end-to-end. Reversed strikes recur throughout musique concrète.

8 · change speed

Change the duration and pitch of a sound together, the way tape does. Slow it down and the pitch drops; speed it up and the pitch rises. This is the original time-and-pitch move from before digital audio existed.

Where in Audacity
Effect → Pitch and Tempo → Change Speed and Pitch…

For most of the twentieth century, most studios had only this kind of time-and-pitch change. If a tape engineer slowed a recording down, the pitch dropped with it. The reading covered the modern decoupled versions (techniques 9 and 10 below). Do the coupled version first.

Exercise

Find a sound from sustain-long/ or texture-continuous/ (something with at least a few seconds of stable sustain). Import it. Select all of it. Apply Effect → Pitch and Tempo → Change Speed and Pitch… In the dialog, set Percent Change to -50 (which both doubles the duration and drops the pitch by exactly an octave). Click Apply. Listen. Then undo (Cmd + Z) and try +100 (which both halves the duration and raises the pitch by exactly an octave). Listen again.

Listen for: the duration and pitch both move together, in the same direction. Slower means lower; faster means higher. The two are coupled, exactly the way they would be if you were physically slowing or speeding a tape reel. Compare this sound with techniques 9 and 10.

9 · time-stretch

Change the duration of a sound without changing its pitch. A signal-processing operation that decouples what tape speed keeps coupled.

Where in Audacity
Effect → Pitch and Tempo → Change Tempo…

Audacity calls this "Change Tempo." A dialog opens; you can change the tempo by a percentage or by entering a new length in seconds. The pitch stays exactly where it was.

Exercise

Use the same sound you just used for Change Speed and Pitch. Either undo (Cmd + Z) until the sound is back to its original, or delete the modified track and re-import the sound fresh. Then select all of it and apply Effect → Pitch and Tempo → Change Tempo… Set Percent Change to -50. Click Apply. Listen.

Listen for: the sound is now twice as long, just like Change Speed and Pitch at -50%. But the pitch is exactly where it was before, not an octave lower.

10 · pitch-shift

Change the pitch of a sound without changing its duration. The complement to time-stretch.

Where in Audacity
Effect → Pitch and Tempo → Change Pitch…

The dialog lets you specify the change in semitones, percent, or as a from-to musical pitch. Semitones match musical intervals: +12 semitones is one octave up, -12 is one octave down.

Exercise

Use the same kind of long-sustain sound (a fresh import, or undo back to the original). Select all of it and apply Effect → Pitch and Tempo → Change Pitch… Set Semitones to -12 (one octave down). Click Apply. Listen. Then undo (Cmd + Z) and try +7 (a perfect fifth up). Listen again.

Listen for: the duration is unchanged, but the pitch has moved. Change Speed and Pitch moves time and pitch together, Change Tempo moves time only, and Change Pitch moves pitch only.
