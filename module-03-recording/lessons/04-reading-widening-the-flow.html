Module 03 · Recording, sample prep & library building

widening the flow

Signal levels, cables, and mic types beyond the basic chain, and the boxes that connect mismatched stages.

Last week we walked the basic recording chain: a dynamic mic, an XLR cable, an audio interface, a computer. In that chain, every stage matches the one before it. Outside the lab you'll find other signal levels, cables, and mics, plus two small boxes that make mismatched stages fit together: the DI box and the hardware preamp.

Today's gear

Take from the lab's gear storage: audio interface, headphones. Plug in and run through the start-of-session steps on the Session Routines card before continuing here.

1 · the three signal levels

The first thing that changes when you swap one source for another is the strength of the electrical signal coming out of it. A dynamic microphone produces a tiny voltage, an electric guitar a stronger one, and a synthesizer a stronger one still. These are three different signal levels, and the names for them are mic level, instrument level, and line level.

Every input on every piece of audio gear is designed for one signal level. An input designed for mic level expects a tiny signal and amplifies it heavily. Plug a line-level signal into that input and the preamp overloads, and the signal distorts and clips. Plug a mic-level signal into a line input and the signal is so far below what the input expects that it disappears into the noise floor. The levels have to match.

The numbers above are estimates. Mic level is around a millivolt (one one-thousandth of a volt), instrument level is around a hundred millivolts, and line level is around a volt. The exact figures depend on the source. A line-level signal is roughly a thousand times stronger than a mic-level signal, a big enough gap that the input circuits for each level are built differently.

1.1 · mic level

Mic level is the signal a microphone puts out. The energy producing it (sound waves vibrating a diaphragm) is small, and so is the signal. A mic-level signal has to be amplified by a preamp before it can be used or recorded. Every audio interface has built-in preamps on its mic inputs. The XLR jack on the front of the PreSonus or the Behringer is a mic input, and the gain knob controls how much that preamp amplifies the signal. Preamps also come outside interfaces: a mixer's channel strip has one on each input, and a standalone hardware preamp is one in a box of its own. We'll come back to those in section 5.

1.2 · instrument level

Instrument level is the signal an electric guitar or bass pickup puts out. A pickup is a small coil of wire wound around a magnet; the steel strings vibrate inside the magnetic field and induce a current in the coil. That mechanism produces a signal that's stronger than a mic puts out but weaker than a typical line-level source. An instrument-level signal also has high impedance, an electrical property that affects how the signal travels through a cable and what kind of input it needs. A high-impedance signal plugged into a low-impedance input (like a line input) loses its high frequencies and sounds dull.

The name is a little misleading

"Instrument level" sounds like it covers all instruments, but it means "what comes out of a pickup." Electric guitars and basses have pickups built in, and they produce instrument-level signals directly. Most other instruments don't. An acoustic guitar, a violin, a saxophone, a piano, your own voice: none of them produces an electrical signal on its own. To capture them you put a mic in front, which gives you mic level.

You can add a pickup to almost any acoustic instrument, though. Aftermarket pickups exist for acoustic guitar, violin, upright bass, mandolin, even brass and wind instruments. With a pickup installed, the instrument has two outputs: its acoustic sound (captured with a mic, at mic level) and the pickup's signal (captured with a cable, at instrument level). Players often record both at once and blend them.

Most audio interfaces have a dedicated instrument input, often marked Hi-Z (for "high impedance") or labeled with a little guitar icon. The lab interfaces handle it in two ways. The PreSonus AudioBox USB 96 has two combo jacks on the front, both labeled Mic/Inst: each one accepts either a mic-level or an instrument-level signal, and the gain knob for that channel has Inst and Mic markings around it that show the typical gain setting for each. On the Behringer U-Phoria UM2, input 1 is a combo jack labeled Mic/Line 1 (mic or line, no instrument), and input 2 is a dedicated 1/4" jack labeled Inst 2 for instruments only.

1.3 · line level

Line level is the signal from a synthesizer, a mixer's main outputs, a CD player's outputs, an audio interface's outputs, or any device whose job is to send audio onward to another device. Gear passes audio from one device to the next at line level: outputs meant to feed other gear send line level, and inputs meant to receive from other gear expect it.

A line-level signal is already strong, and the preamp behind a line input applies far less gain than the one behind a mic input. The lab interfaces don't have dedicated line inputs, but they can accept a line-level signal on the 1/4" side of a combo jack (the Behringer is explicit about it, with its input 1 labeled Mic/Line 1). Larger interfaces with more channels often dedicate certain inputs to line level only.

Line level comes in two flavors. Consumer line level (called -10 dBV, the spec a CD player or a typical home stereo uses) is around 0.3 volts. Professional line level (called +4 dBu, used in studio gear and pro mixers) is around 1.2 volts, four times stronger. Plug a consumer-line source into a pro-line input and the signal is quieter than the input expects; plug a pro-line source into a consumer-line input and the signal is too hot. The lab interfaces handle both, but mismatched line levels are a common source of "why is this signal so quiet" (or so loud) in mixed-gear setups.

2 · cables for each level

Four cable types cover almost everything you'll use in audio work: XLR for mic and balanced line, TS for instrument, TRS for balanced line or unbalanced stereo, and RCA for consumer-line connections. The cable type also hints at what kind of signal is on it.

2.1 · XLR (recap)

The XLR cable from Lecture 1 carries mic-level signals from microphones to interface inputs. It has three pins and three conductors inside, and it's balanced: two out-of-phase copies of the signal and a ground. The balanced design lets the receiving end cancel out any electromagnetic noise picked up along the cable's run. On a weak mic-level signal, any added noise would be obvious.

XLR cables can also carry balanced line-level signals between professional audio gear. A studio preamp's output feeding a mixer's input travels over XLR at line level, while a microphone feeding an interface travels over the same kind of cable at mic level.

How can one cable carry two different signal levels?

A cable is passive: conductors and a shield, with no electronics inside. Whatever voltage the device at one end puts onto the wires, the device at the other end sees. A mic-level signal (a millivolt or so) and a line-level signal (a volt or so) are both voltages swinging up and down, and the cable carries them the same way.

The gear at each end sets the level. A microphone's transducer puts out mic level, and a studio preamp's output circuit is designed to put out line level. The cable is the path between them. The balanced noise-rejection trick works at either level: it depends on the cable's two signal wires, not on how strong the signal is.

2.2 · TS

A TS cable ends in a quarter-inch plug with two metal sections separated by a single insulating ring: the tip (T) and the sleeve (S). Inside the cable there are two conductors: one for the signal, one for the ground and shield. It's the everyday guitar cable, the kind that plugs a guitar into an amplifier or into the Hi-Z input on an audio interface.

TS is unbalanced. There's only one copy of the signal traveling down the cable, with the ground returning the current. With one copy, the balanced-cable trick from Lecture 1 (two out-of-phase copies, one flipped back and summed with the other at the far end, where the noise cancels) can't happen. Any electromagnetic noise the cable picks up along its run goes straight into the signal. A longer guitar cable picks up more of it: a 50-foot guitar cable run across a room full of stage lights produces a noticeable hum.

Instrument-level signals are strong enough (around 100 millivolts) that a little picked-up noise stays small relative to the signal, and a typical TS run is much shorter than a typical XLR run, with less cable to pick up noise. Carrying a mic-level signal, the same cable would have an unacceptable signal-to-noise ratio. Mics use balanced XLR instead.

2.3 · TRS

A TRS cable ends in a quarter-inch plug that looks like a TS plug with one extra ring: the tip (T), ring (R), and sleeve (S). It has three sections and three conductors inside. The third conductor gives the cable two uses, and the gear at each end decides which use applies.

Used balanced, the three conductors are wired the same way as an XLR's: signal, inverted signal, ground. At the receiving end, the inverted copy is flipped and summed with the original; noise picked up along the cable cancels. This is how a TRS cable carries balanced line-level signals between professional gear. The PreSonus AudioBox's Main Out jacks are TRS and send balanced line level to studio monitors. (Take a look at the back of the lab's PreSonus and you'll see them.)

Used unbalanced, the three conductors carry a stereo signal: left, right, and ground. Headphones use TRS this way. A pair of headphones has two speakers (one for each ear), each needing its own signal, plus a shared ground. The TRS plug at the end of a headphone cable carries left on the tip, right on the ring, and ground on the sleeve.

Between a mixer's balanced output and a studio monitor's balanced input, a TRS cable carries balanced mono. Between an interface's headphone jack and a pair of headphones, it carries unbalanced stereo.

A word on mono and stereo

A mono signal is one channel of audio: one stream of sound, one waveform. A stereo signal is two channels, conventionally called left and right, played back through two speakers (or the two sides of a pair of headphones) to give the listener a sense of where sounds are positioned in space. Stereo is two complete audio signals traveling alongside each other, not a single signal split in two.

A single TRS cable can carry balanced mono or unbalanced stereo, but never both at once. Three conductors, two ways to spend them: in balanced mono they're (signal+, signal-, ground), one channel with an inverted copy that cancels noise at the receiver; in unbalanced stereo they're (left, right, ground), two channels with no spare conductor for the noise-canceling copy of either one. To send stereo with the noise rejection of balanced, you run two cables, one balanced mono for the left channel and one balanced mono for the right. (The PreSonus has two separate TRS Main Out jacks labeled L and R: each one is a balanced mono output, and together they make a balanced stereo pair.)

2.4 · RCA

An RCA cable ends in a small round plug with a center pin and an outer metal sleeve, designed to push straight into a matching jack rather than locking the way XLR does. RCA cables are unbalanced (no noise-rejection trick) and carry one channel of audio per plug. For stereo, you use two of them, conventionally color-coded: red for the right channel, white (or black) for the left.

RCA is the connector on most consumer audio gear: home stereos, CD players, turntables, older DVD players, the back of a TV. On most of that gear it carries consumer line level (-10 dBV, the lower of the two line-level flavors from section 1.3). A turntable is the exception: its RCA output is a much weaker phono-level signal, and it needs a phono preamp to bring it up to line level. Studios sometimes use RCA too, mostly to connect consumer gear (a CD player, or a turntable into its phono preamp) to a professional setup. Consumer line level is lower than pro-line gear expects.

Too many cables?

There are a lot of cables for one job (moving audio around), and each type handles a specific problem. XLR is balanced, and it rejects the noise a long mic run picks up. TS is a cheap, simple cable for guitar signals, which are strong enough not to need balancing. TRS uses the same quarter-inch connector for balanced runs between pro gear and for stereo to headphones. RCA is the cheap push-on connector of home audio. What you need to know is which cable plugs into which jack and what signal is on it, and the labels on a piece of gear (Mic, Line, Inst, +48V) tell you most of that.

XLR locks into place, and a cable someone trips over on stage stays plugged in. Quarter-inch plugs go in and out fast for guitarists who swap cables constantly, and RCA pushes on and is cheap to manufacture. In the lab you'll find one practical crossover: a TS plug fits into a TRS jack (and the reverse), and audio flows. The TS plug shorts the ring to the sleeve, and the connection becomes unbalanced. An XLR plug doesn't fit a quarter-inch jack at all.

On cost: cables span a huge price range, and the audible differences across that range are smaller than the marketing suggests. A 10-foot TS guitar cable from a reputable maker (Hosa, Live Wire, Monoprice) costs around $10 to $15 and works fine. A 10-foot XLR mic cable from the same makers is $15 to $25. Boutique cables (Mogami, Canare for the cable itself; Neutrik for connectors) cost more, are better built, and last longer in heavy use. Many professional studios use them. Cables priced in the hundreds or thousands tend to trade on claims about audio quality that don't hold up to blind tests. For this course, well-made budget cables are enough.

3 · other mic types

The mics in the lab are dynamics, and you'll use them for the paper recordings and most of your sample-library work. Outside the lab you'll find two other transducer types in most studios: the condenser and, less often, the ribbon. Like a dynamic, both convert acoustic energy into electrical energy. Each uses a different mechanism inside, and each mechanism gives a different sound.

3.1 · the condenser microphone

Inside a condenser mic, instead of a coil-and-magnet assembly (like the one we saw in the dynamic mic last week), there are two thin metal plates held very close together, with one of them free to move. Sound waves push the movable plate back and forth, changing the distance between the two plates. The mic measures that changing distance as a changing electrical capacitance, and turns it into an output signal.

With no coil attached, the movable plate is far lighter than a dynamic mic's diaphragm. A lighter plate responds more readily to quiet sounds and to fast detail (the high-frequency transients in a hi-hat hit, the breath at the start of a sung consonant, the air around a soft acoustic guitar). Condensers capture those details with a clarity that dynamics smooth over. Studios often put condensers on vocals, acoustic guitar, piano, drum overheads, and anything you want to sound detailed and present.

The trade-off is that the condenser's two-plate design needs a small electrical charge across the plates to work at all. An audio interface (or a mixer, or a dedicated mic preamp) can send a small DC voltage up the same XLR cable that carries the audio signal back down. This is called phantom power, usually +48 volts, and the button or switch labeled +48V on an interface turns it on. Turn it on when you plug in a condenser; turn it off when you plug in a dynamic.

Phantom power and dynamic mics

If you forget to switch phantom power off, it won't damage a modern dynamic mic with a balanced XLR output. The +48V appears equally on both signal pins, and the mic's circuit ignores it. Older or unusual designs (some ribbon mics in particular) can be damaged. Switch phantom power on only when you know the mic needs it.

3.2 · the ribbon microphone

A ribbon mic uses a thin strip of corrugated metal foil suspended in a magnetic field. Sound waves move the ribbon back and forth in the field, and the moving ribbon generates a small current the same way a dynamic mic's coil does. The ribbon is much lighter than a coil, and it captures detail more like a condenser. Most ribbon mics don't need phantom power, and some older designs can be damaged by it.

Ribbons have a smooth, slightly darker character that engineers use on certain sources: brass instruments, guitar amplifiers, voiceover work. They're also fragile. A strong blast of air (a kick drum up close, someone speaking into the mic from a few inches away) or a careless drop can stretch the ribbon and ruin the mic. You won't use ribbons in this course.

3.3 · choosing between mic types

Your Module 3 lab recordings use the dynamic mic at your station, which needs no phantom power.

Engineers have rough starting points for where each type goes. Dynamic mics handle loud sources without distorting and take physical abuse well. They end up in front of kick drums, snare drums, electric guitar amps, and brass instruments, and on stage in front of vocalists who handle their mic roughly. Condensers go on the quiet, detailed sources from section 3.1. They need phantom power, and they don't tolerate being dropped. Ribbon mics go on sources where a dynamic would sound harsh and a condenser too forward: brass, electric guitar amps in some recording styles, voiceover and broadcast work. None of these are rules, and plenty of great recordings have been made with the "wrong" mic for the job.

Prices span a wide range. A reliable dynamic (Shure SM57, Shure SM58, Sennheiser e835) costs around $100 new and lasts for decades. Decent condensers start around $100 to $300 for entry-level large-diaphragm models (Rode NT1-A, Audio-Technica AT2020, AKG P220), step up to $500 to $1,500 for studio mainstays (Rode NT2-A, AKG C414, Neumann TLM 102), and reach into the thousands for the famous ones (the Neumann U87 in the photo above, around $3,500). Ribbon mics are more delicate to manufacture and tend to start higher than condensers: $300 to $700 for entry-level (Cascade Fat Head, sE Electronics X1R), $1,000 to $1,500 for the Royer R-121 (the one in the photo), and up from there for vintage models or boutique reissues. For everything in this course, and for most home-studio and project-studio work, a $100 dynamic and a $200 condenser cover almost any need you'll have. The expensive mics pay for themselves in professional studios, where the cost is spread across thousands of sessions. You don't need one to make good recordings.

People in audio have strong, specific opinions about individual mic models, and you'll hear those opinions everywhere (YouTube reviews, forum threads, the engineer at the studio you visit, the producer your friend works with). "You need a U87 for that vocal." "Nothing beats a 421 on toms." "The R-121 is the only ribbon I'd reach for." Some of this is hard-earned knowledge from people who've A/B-compared dozens of mics over decades. Some of it is brand identity, peer pressure, and the human tendency to defend the gear we already own. You don't need to take sides in any of these debates yet. As you record more, you'll develop your own ears for what each mic does and which one you want in which situation.

4 · polar patterns beyond cardioid

The dynamic mics in the lab are cardioid: most sensitive in front, less sensitive at the sides, almost deaf to sound coming from directly behind. That's one polar pattern. Many microphones, especially condensers, can switch between several patterns. Three are common: cardioid, omnidirectional, and figure-8 (also called bi-directional). A handful of other patterns are variants of these.

Cardioid rejects what's behind it and emphasizes what's in front. It's common in live work, where you want the mic to pick up the source and not the room (or the monitor speaker pointed at the performer). Most dynamic mics have only this pattern, and most condensers are cardioid too.

Omnidirectional picks up equally from every direction, a sphere around the mic. It's useful when you want the room to be part of the recording (an orchestra at a respectful distance, a piano in a hall, an ambience track for a sound design library) or when the source moves around (a conversation between two people across a table, where pointing the mic at one of them would lose the other).

Figure-8 picks up equally from front and back and rejects sound from the sides: two equal lobes. It's useful for capturing two sources facing each other across a mic (a duet, an interview), or for stereo mic'ing techniques that combine a cardioid and a figure-8 to capture a wide image of a single source.

The other three patterns in the chart are variants. Super-cardioid and hyper-cardioid are tighter, more directional versions of cardioid that trade a little rejection at the back for tighter focus at the front; they're common on stage mics that need extra isolation. Shotgun is the most directional of all, a narrow forward lobe used in film and television production to pick up dialogue from a distance.

Most condensers labeled "multi-pattern" let you switch between cardioid, omnidirectional, and figure-8 (and sometimes a few intermediate patterns) with a small switch on the mic body. Choosing a polar pattern is a creative decision about what to include and what to exclude, like choosing the mic or where to place it.

5 · signal modifiers: DI boxes and hardware preamps

Each input expects one signal level: mic, instrument, or line. When the source you have and the input you have don't match, you insert a signal modifier: a small box that takes a signal at one level and outputs it at another. Two common ones are the DI box and the hardware preamp.

5.1 · the DI box

"DI" stands for direct injection, or sometimes just direct input. A DI box takes an unbalanced instrument-level signal (the kind that comes out of a guitar) and outputs a balanced mic-level signal (the kind a mic input expects). It lowers the signal level and converts unbalanced to balanced in one step.

Imagine a band playing live in a venue with a long stage. The guitarist's amp is at the back of the stage; the mixer is at the back of the venue, fifty feet away. The guitarist wants to send a signal from their guitar (or from a pedal output) all the way to the mixer. An unbalanced TS cable that long picks up enough electromagnetic interference to bury the signal. Instead, plug the guitar into a DI box at the stage end and run a long XLR cable from the DI's output to the mixer. The balanced cable rejects the noise along the way. At the mixer, the signal enters a mic input as if it had come from a microphone.

In the studio, to record a bass guitar directly into a mixer that has no instrument inputs, plug the bass into a DI and the DI into a mic input. A passive DI box needs no power: a transformer inside makes the level change. An active DI box is powered, often by phantom power from the mic input it feeds, and has extra circuitry for the impedance change.

5.2 · the hardware preamp

A preamp takes a mic-level signal and amplifies it to line level. Audio interfaces have built-in preamps on their mic inputs (the gain knob controls them). A hardware preamp is the same circuit in a standalone box, often built with design choices that give the amplification a distinctive sound.

A high-quality hardware preamp can be quieter (lower self-noise) and more transparent than the budget preamps in a $100 audio interface. For very quiet sources recorded with sensitive condenser mics, the difference can be audible.

Some preamp designs also add a coloration to the signal as they amplify it: a slight harmonic distortion that engineers describe as warmth, punch, saturation, or thickness. The Neve 1073 (a preamp originally designed in the 1970s for British studio consoles) and the API 312 (designed in the same era for American consoles) are two preamps known for this character. Engineers choose them for the way their coloration flatters certain sources, and that coloration is what people mean by "the Neve sound" or "the API sound."

The preamps built into the lab's interfaces are clean and quiet, and they're all you need for this course. In studios, "what preamp did you track this on?" is a common question, and the choice changes the sound.

5.3 · the widened chain

With a DI box and a hardware preamp, the basic recording chain from Lecture 1 adapts to almost any source. The diagram below shows four paths into the same audio interface: a mic recording (the basic chain you already know), a mic recording through a hardware preamp into a line input (where the preamp does the amplification instead of the interface), a guitar recording through the interface's instrument input (no DI needed), and a guitar recording through a DI box into a mic input (for gear without an instrument input).

The chain is a sequence of matching steps. At every boundary between two devices, the signal level on the cable matches the input expecting it. When a source produces a signal at the wrong level for the input you have, a signal modifier closes the gap. The DI box is one case (instrument level becomes balanced mic level); a hardware preamp is another (mic level becomes line level).

With that pattern, you can reason through any setup. A synth into a mixer's line input: line out, line in, levels match, done. A guitar into a mic input: levels don't match, and you need a DI between them. A condenser mic into an interface sends mic level on XLR for the interface's preamp to amplify, and the mic also needs phantom power, so the +48V button has to be on.

vocabulary

Signal level
The strength of the electrical signal a device produces or expects. Three levels matter in audio work: mic level (weakest, around 1 millivolt), instrument level (around 100 millivolts), and line level (strongest, around 1 volt). Every input is designed for one level; mismatched levels produce noise, distortion, or silence.
Mic level
The signal level produced by any microphone. Weak, around 1 millivolt. Needs a preamp before it can be used or recorded. Travels on XLR cables, balanced.
Instrument level
The signal level produced by electric guitar and bass pickups. Stronger than mic level, weaker than line level, around 100 millivolts. Also high-impedance, and needs a dedicated Hi-Z input. Travels on TS cables, unbalanced.
Line level
The signal level produced by synthesizers, mixers, audio-interface outputs, and most pieces of audio gear designed to send signal onward. Strong, around 1 volt. Travels on TRS cables (balanced) or RCA cables (unbalanced consumer); also on XLR (balanced professional). Comes in two flavors: consumer (-10 dBV, around 0.3 V) and professional (+4 dBu, around 1.2 V).
Impedance
An electrical property of an input or output that affects how a signal travels and how it sounds. High-impedance signals (like a guitar pickup) need high-impedance inputs (the Hi-Z input on an interface). Plugging a high-impedance signal into a low-impedance input loses the high frequencies. A DI box converts high-impedance instrument level to low-impedance mic level.
Hi-Z
Short for "high impedance." A label on audio-interface inputs designed to receive instrument-level signals from electric guitars or basses. Some interfaces use a guitar icon instead. The lab interfaces handle this in two ways: the PreSonus marks both combo jacks Mic/Inst, while the Behringer has a separate Inst 2 jack dedicated to instruments.
TS cable
A quarter-inch cable with two conductors and a connector that has a tip and a sleeve separated by one insulating ring. Carries unbalanced instrument-level signals. The everyday guitar cable.
TRS cable
A quarter-inch cable with three conductors and a connector that has a tip, a ring, and a sleeve separated by two insulating rings. Can carry balanced line-level (one signal, noise-rejecting) or unbalanced stereo (two signals plus ground). What it's doing depends on what's plugged in at each end. Headphone cables are TRS carrying stereo; pro studio interconnects are TRS carrying balanced mono.
RCA cable
An unbalanced cable with a push-on connector that has a center pin and an outer sleeve. Carries one channel of audio per plug, so stereo uses two (red = right, white or black = left). The connector on most consumer audio gear, where it carries consumer line level (-10 dBV). A turntable's RCA output is phono level, which needs a phono preamp.
Balanced cable
A cable that carries two out-of-phase copies of the signal so the receiving end can cancel out picked-up noise. XLR is always balanced; TRS is balanced when used for mono interconnects; TS is never balanced.
Unbalanced cable
A cable that carries a single copy of the signal plus a ground return. Simpler and cheaper than balanced, but vulnerable to noise picked up along the cable's run. TS, RCA, and TRS-used-as-stereo are all unbalanced.
Mono
A signal made of one audio channel. One waveform, one stream of sound. The signal a single mic produces is mono. A signal sent over a balanced TRS or XLR cable between pro gear is mono (the second conductor is an inverted copy of the same channel, not a different one).
Stereo
A signal made of two audio channels, conventionally called left and right, played back through two speakers (or the two sides of a pair of headphones) to give the listener a sense of where sounds are positioned in space. Two complete audio signals traveling alongside each other, not one signal split in two. A TRS cable carrying stereo is unbalanced: its three conductors are spent on left, right, and ground, with none left over for the noise-canceling inverted copy.
Condenser microphone
A mic that uses two close-spaced metal plates instead of a coil and magnet. The plate spacing changes when sound hits the diaphragm, and the mic measures that change as a changing capacitance. More sensitive to detail and high frequencies than a dynamic. Needs phantom power.
Ribbon microphone
A mic that uses a thin strip of corrugated metal foil suspended in a magnetic field. Captures detail like a condenser but usually doesn't need phantom power. Has a smooth, slightly dark character. Fragile; not used in the lab.
Phantom power
A small DC voltage (conventionally +48 volts) sent up an XLR cable from an interface or mixer to power a condenser mic. The +48V switch on an audio interface turns it on or off. Turn it on when using a condenser; off when using a dynamic (or any other mic that doesn't need it).
Polar pattern
The shape of a microphone's sensitivity to sound from different directions. Three patterns are common: cardioid (front), omnidirectional (all directions equally), and figure-8 (front and back equally, rejecting the sides). Super-cardioid, hyper-cardioid, and shotgun are variants. Many condenser mics have a switch that selects between patterns.
Omnidirectional
A polar pattern that picks up sound equally from every direction. Useful when the room is part of the recording or when the source moves around the mic.
Figure-8
A polar pattern that picks up equally from front and back but rejects the sides. Also called bi-directional. Useful for capturing two sources facing each other across a mic, or as one half of a stereo mic'ing technique.
Super-cardioid, hyper-cardioid, shotgun
Three variants of the cardioid pattern that trade rejection at the rear for tighter focus at the front. Super- and hyper-cardioid are common on stage mics that need extra isolation from monitors and bleed; shotgun is the most directional, used to pick up dialogue at a distance in film and television production.
DI box
A signal modifier (short for "direct injection") that takes an unbalanced instrument-level signal and outputs a balanced mic-level signal. Lets you plug a guitar into a mic input, and lets you send an instrument signal long distances without picking up noise. Passive DIs use a transformer; active DIs are powered (sometimes by phantom power from the input they feed).
Hardware preamp
A standalone preamp circuit in its own box, separate from an audio interface. Takes mic level and outputs line level, often with a deliberate sonic coloration (warmth, punch, saturation) that engineers use as a creative tool. The Neve 1073 and the API 312 are two examples known for their character.
Signal modifier
A general term for the small boxes that convert a signal from one level or format to another to fit the next stage of a chain. The DI box and the hardware preamp are two examples.
