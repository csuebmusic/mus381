1 · meet the band: The Sound Below

The Sound Below is a five-piece: a mic-level vocal, a mic-level guitar amp, a bass captured through a DI box and a mic on its amp (the DI setup from Lecture 2), a four-mic drum setup (kick, snare, and a stereo pair of overhead condensers), and a stage keyboardist with a laptop running alongside (the only line-level signals in the band). That's twelve signals. The console has sixteen input channels, and the band fills three-quarters of them.

Why the bass gets two channels

Bass is the one source on the band today captured two ways at once. The bassist's signal goes into a DI box first: the box's direct out sends a clean, balanced copy straight to the console (the "dry" signal), and the box's thru jack passes the instrument signal along to the bass amp, where a dynamic mic on the bass amp's speaker captures the amp's tone (the "wet" signal). That's two channels and two mic-level XLRs into the console. The amp mic's signal arrives a little later than the DI's: sound in air covers about a foot per millisecond. During the show the engineer blends them: the DI gives the low-end punch and the note attack, and the mic'd amp gives the warmth, the room, and the speaker's character. DI alone often sounds sterile, and amp alone often sounds muddy.

Why the keyboardist's signals look different

Apart from the bass DI, the keyboardist's four signals are the band's only non-microphone sources, and they're its only line-level signals. The stage keyboard puts out a stereo pair from its main outputs (L and R, 1/4" TRS, line level); the laptop puts out a second stereo pair from its audio interface (also L and R, 1/4" TRS, line level).

Line-level signals are already strong enough to pass through a console's circuitry without a preamp's amplification. A mic preamp boosts weak mic-level signals up to the level the rest of the console expects, and a line-level signal sent into a mic preamp without precaution can be over-amplified and clip. Live sound handles line-level sources one way (below); the studio handout (Lab 4) covers the other.

2 · Scenario 1 · live sound

The Sound Below is playing a small venue. The stage holds the band, the amps, the drums, the keys rig, and five stage monitors (one in front of each performer). Out in front of the stage are the front-of-house speakers facing the audience. The engineer is halfway back in the room, behind the console.

The job: take the twelve signals coming off the stage and send them to two destinations at once.

  1. The front of house (FOH) speakers face the audience, who need to hear the whole band balanced as a mix. The console builds the master mix and sends it out at MASTER O/P, which cables to the FOH power amps that drive the audience speakers.
  2. The stage monitors each get a separate mix for one performer, with what that player needs to hear to perform. The vocalist mostly wants their own voice plus a bit of the kick to lock the timing; the bassist wants kick, snare, their own bass, and a little vocal; the drummer wants the bass and the vocal; the guitarist wants vocal, guitar, and drums; the keyboardist wants vocal, her own keys and laptop, and enough drums to lock in.

In the diagram below, the band's twelve signals come in on the left: eight mic-level signals on XLR (vocal, guitar amp, bass DI, bass amp, four drum mics) and four line-level signals on TRS from the keyboardist (keys L/R, laptop L/R), which pass through a DI box on their way to the console. On the right, the console's output goes to two destinations: the front-of-house speakers facing the audience, and the five stage monitors facing the performers.

The engineer's job for the night is to ride the faders: listen continuously to what's coming out of the FOH speakers, keep the band balanced as the song moves (vocal up in the chorus, guitar back during the verse, kick steady through it all), and at the same time keep the five stage monitor mixes balanced for each player on stage. The engineer makes small fader moves and EQ adjustments in real time throughout the show, until the band walks off.

from the stage to the console

Before any routing, the twelve signals have to physically travel from the stage to the console. Venues use two pieces of dedicated hardware for this: a stage box and a snake.

At the side of the stage, on the floor or bolted to a riser, is a sturdy metal box about the size of a thick laptop. Its top face is a grid of jacks: a row of female XLR inputs, labeled 1, 2, 3, ... up to the number of channels on the box. This is the stage box. Every mic on the stage plugs into one of its jacks with a short XLR cable. The longest cable on the stage at this point is the one from the drum overhead to the stage box, maybe ten feet.

From the back of the stage box, one fat black cable runs off the stage, along the wall or under the floor, all the way to the console at front-of-house. This cable is the snake (sometimes a multicore, or in audio-engineer slang the multi). Inside its outer jacket are dozens of individually shielded balanced pairs: one pair per channel of the stage box, often plus a few return pairs going back the other way for monitor speakers on the stage. A 16-channel snake carries 16 signals on a single physical cable; a 32-channel snake carries 32. At the console end, the snake terminates in a fan-out: a bundle of individual XLR connectors, labeled 1 through N, that plug into the back of the console.

The stage box jack number becomes the console channel number. Vocalist's mic plugged into stage box jack 1 arrives on console channel 1. Kick mic plugged into jack 5 arrives on console channel 5. Re-patch the mic into a different jack and the source moves to a different console channel; the engineer at FOH has to re-label the fader.

Every input on the band plugs straight into the stage box except the keyboardist's. The stage box has female XLR jacks; the keyboard and the laptop have 1/4" TRS line outputs. The connectors don't match. A stage box can carry a line-level signal on a balanced XLR pair, but then the preamp gain on that channel at the console has to be set much lower than for a mic: a line-level signal hitting a preamp turned up for mic level clips. A DI box, the same kind that handled the bass guitar in Lecture 2, handles both the connector and the level.

The keyboardist has a four-channel DI box at her station. Her stage keyboard's L and R outputs go into two of the DI's 1/4" TRS inputs; the laptop's L and R outputs go into the other two. On the DI box's front face, the pad switch for each channel is engaged. The pad lowers the hot line-level input before it reaches the DI's internal circuit, which would otherwise clip. Four XLR cables carry four mic-level signals from the DI box's outputs to four jacks on the stage box. On the snake, the keyboardist's four channels are the same as the eight mic channels: balanced, mic-level, on XLR. The engineer at FOH treats them as four more mic inputs with their preamps set a touch lower than for an actual mic.

Two simpler alternatives, when they're available

The DI-box route brings every channel to the console as XLR on the MIC jack.

The first alternative is a TRS-to-XLR cable. The signal stays balanced and line-level (TRS and XLR can both carry a balanced signal; a properly wired adapter cable remaps the conductors), and the keys arrive at the stage box on the XLR jack at line level instead of mic level. The console's preamp on that channel gets set much lower than for a mic. Most consoles' MIC jacks accept up to about +4 dBu, and this works on most of them.

For the second, many stage boxes (and the consoles they feed into) have a separate row of 1/4" TRS line-level inputs alongside the XLR row, for keys, drum machines, and other line-level sources. When the stage box has these, the keys plug straight in with regular TRS cables and arrive at the console on the LINE jack at line level. The ProX in the photo above doesn't have them; many touring rigs do.

Modern digital snakes

The picture above describes an analog snake: one shielded pair per audio channel, lots of copper, heavy. A modern variant is the digital snake: a small box at the stage that does analog-to-digital conversion locally and sends all the channels as a single digital stream over an ethernet cable (or a fiber-optic cable) to the console. A digital snake feeds a digital console with a matching digital input. An analog console (like the Toft ATB in MB2508) expects analog audio on XLR jacks at every input and needs an analog snake. The same Cat6 cable that runs your laptop's internet can carry 32 channels of audio at 48 kHz. The connectors are different and the stage box also converts analog to digital, but the idea is the same: many signals bundled onto one path. Many touring engineers prefer digital snakes for the weight savings; many older venues still use the analog snakes they already own, which last for decades.

the FOH mix: routing to the master mix

Patch 1 · Vocal, guitar, bass DI, bass amp (channels 1-4) directly to the master mix.

  1. On each of channels 1, 2, 3, and 4, press the L-R button down. The 1-2, 3-4, 5-6, and 7-8 buttons stay up.
  2. On each of those channels, set the channel pan knob (center for vocal and bass DI, slightly left or right for the two amp mics if the engineer wants width).
  3. Adjust each channel's INPUT GAIN, EQ, and fader as the band plays.

Channels 1-4 now feed the master mix directly, each on its own fader.

Patch 2 · Drums (channels 5-8) routed to subgroup pair 1-2, then into the master mix.

  1. On each of channels 5 (kick), 6 (snare), 7 (overhead L), and 8 (overhead R), press the 1-2 button down. L-R stays up.
  2. Pan the kick and snare center, overhead L hard left, and overhead R hard right. The four channels now sum into the subgroup pair (sub 1 = left side of the kit, sub 2 = right).
  3. On submaster strips 1 and 2, set the TAPE button up (the live subgroup is the source, not playback).
  4. On submaster strip 1, turn MON LEVEL up and set MON PAN hard left. On submaster strip 2, turn MON LEVEL up and set MON PAN hard right. The drum kit now arrives in the master mix as a stereo pair.
  5. Leave both submaster faders down. The submaster faders set the level at the SUBMASTER OUTPUTS jacks (the DAW send), and nothing is patched to those jacks in this scenario.

The engineer's drum-control surface is now two knobs: MON LEVEL on submaster 1 (drum bus left), MON LEVEL on submaster 2 (drum bus right). Pull both down by 3 dB during a quiet verse and the whole kit drops by 3 dB in the master mix; the internal balance among the drum mics doesn't move.

Patch 3 · Keys and laptop (channels 9-12) routed to subgroup pair 3-4, then into the master mix.

  1. On each of channels 9 (keys L), 10 (keys R), 11 (laptop L), and 12 (laptop R), press the 3-4 button down. L-R stays up.
  2. Pan channels 9 and 11 hard left and channels 10 and 12 hard right. The two stereo pairs sum across the subgroup pair into one stereo electronic submix.
  3. On submaster strips 3 and 4, set TAPE up and MON LEVEL up, with MON PAN hard left on sub 3 and hard right on sub 4. The electronic submix now arrives in the master mix.
  4. Leave submaster faders 3 and 4 down.

One pair of knobs (MON LEVEL on submasters 3 and 4) controls everything coming from the keys rig in the FOH mix. The internal balance between the stage keyboard and the laptop stays set wherever the keyboardist's gain-staging at her station put it.

the stage monitor mixes: building five different mixes on the aux sends

Each of the five performers needs a different blend in their stage monitor, and each blend is built on an aux send. Each aux is its own bus, with its own master level, going out the back of the console at the AUX MASTERS jacks (six 1/4" TRS jacks, balanced, line-level, one per aux).

Getting that line-level signal back to the stage takes the same kind of cabling you saw on the input side, run in reverse. Most analog snakes have a few return lines in addition to their input channels: balanced pairs that travel from the FOH end back to the stage box. The engineer patches an AUX MASTERS jack into one return line at the console end; the same signal appears at the stage box on a corresponding return connector (usually XLR), and from there a short cable runs to the stage monitor. Five auxes need five return lines. The ProX snake in the photo has four (A-D), and a fifth monitor feed would need its own cable run or a snake with more returns.

What's at the end of that cable depends on the monitor. A powered stage monitor has the amp built into the cabinet: the cable plugs directly into the monitor's balanced input (usually XLR, or sometimes TRS) and the line-level signal drives the speaker through the internal amp. A passive stage monitor needs an external amp in between: the cable plugs into the amp's line input, the amp boosts line-level up to speaker-level, and a thick speaker cable carries that to the monitor itself. Either way the AUX MASTERS jack delivers the same line-level signal; what changes is how many boxes are between the jack and the speaker cone.

Each performer gets one aux (auxes 1 through 5, one per stage monitor). The pattern below is the same for all five; only the channels turned up on each aux change.

Patch · One stage monitor mix on one aux.

  1. Pick an aux (1, 2, 3, 4, or 5) for this performer's mix.
  2. Set that aux to pre-fader on every input channel. Aux 1 is permanently pre-fader (no switch). For Auxes 2 through 5, press the channel's PRE button down for that aux. Pre-fader means the aux send level is independent of the channel fader: the FOH engineer can pull the vocal fader down between songs and the singer still hears their own voice in their stage monitor at the same level.
  3. On the channels this performer needs to hear, turn the aux knob up to a working level. On the channels they don't need, leave the aux knob at zero.
  4. Adjust each channel's contribution by ear (the performer's own voice or instrument usually loud, the time-keeping references like kick and snare audible enough to lock in, everything else minimal or off).
  5. At the master section, set the Aux Master knob for this aux to a working line-level send (about unity, then trim by ear once the monitor is playing).

Repeat the patch five times, once per performer. The blends below are a starting point for The Sound Below; adjust to taste once the band is playing.
