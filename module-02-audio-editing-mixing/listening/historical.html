Module 02 · Digital audio, editing & mixing

listening: musique concrète

Three pieces from the musique concrète tradition. Listen with the editing vocabulary in mind.

Project 1 asks you to build a piece from existing sound material: to treat sound as something you cut and arrange, rather than something you perform live or notate on a staff. This is the idea behind musique concrète, a tradition started in Paris in 1948 by Pierre Schaeffer. He recorded everyday sounds (trains, doors, voices, water) and worked with them as raw material, the way a sculptor works with stone.

before you listen: a brief history

The composers who started musique concrète were working decades before digital audio existed. Pierre Schaeffer's Études de bruits (1948), the first works in the genre, were cut directly to acetate disc. Usable magnetic tape recorders weren't yet widely available in France. By the early 1950s tape had taken over, and for the next thirty years, this tradition was a tape tradition.

Composers built their pieces by physically splicing lengths of magnetic tape with razor blades, looping them, slowing them down or speeding them up, and playing them backward. An edit meant laying the tape in a metal splicing block, cutting it with a blade, and joining the ends with a piece of adhesive splicing tape.

The artifacts you might hear in these pieces (surface noise, slight pitch instability, the audible seam of an edit) aren't flaws in the recording. They're the sound of the medium itself: magnetic tape and disc.

Computer-based music synthesis began in research labs as early as the 1950s, but the equipment was expensive and limited. Tape stayed dominant through the 1970s. The first commercial digital samplers went on sale around 1979, and they cost as much as a house. Through the 1980s and into the early 1990s, studios began mixing tape and digital workflows. Hard-disk recording gradually became affordable. By the late 1990s, anyone with a personal computer and a little software could do digitally what had previously required a tape studio. Within another decade, working in tape became a deliberate aesthetic choice rather than a default.

Schaeffer's and Henry's pieces were physical objects played on machines: discs, and lengths of tape with cuts in them. Keep the medium in mind as you listen, and listen as someone who is about to make a piece in the same tradition.

questions

Answer all four.

  1. Describe the source sounds in one of the historical pieces (Schaeffer or Henry). What are they? How recognizable are they?
  2. Pick one of the historical pieces and describe how it's structured over time. Does it build? Repeat? Vary? What makes it hold together as a piece rather than a random collection of sounds?
  3. Across the three pieces, identify one specific compositional or editing technique you noticed (repetition, layering, sudden silence, pitch-shifting, stuttering, etc.) and describe how it's used.
  4. Based on this listening, what's one specific thing you want to try in your own Project 1? Be concrete, as in "I want to try repeating a single sound at different speeds."

a note on listening

Use headphones if you can. Listen all the way through at least once before answering, and give each piece your full attention. If a piece feels strange or boring at first, keep listening. On a second listen you'll hear edits and layers you missed the first time.
