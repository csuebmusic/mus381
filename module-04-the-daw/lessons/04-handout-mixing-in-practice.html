mixing in Ableton

Bounce a finished session to audio, then mix it.

The session for this week is on the class server: a longer piece with several instruments, some played from MIDI, some recorded as audio. You bounce it to audio first, then mix it. Mixing means balancing the parts so you can hear each one clearly in the whole. You did this on the Toft console in Module 3, and now you do it in Live's mixer.

Today's gear

Take from the lab's gear storage: audio interface and headphones. Leave the MIDI keyboard in storage today. Run through the start-of-session steps on the Session Routines card before continuing here.

  3. Bounce the MIDI tracks to audio.
  4. Consolidate every track into one clip.
  5. Mix: levels, pan, groups, inserts (EQ, compression, a limiter), and shared sends for reverb and delay.

get the session

During the start-of-session download, click the / at the top of the directory tree on the right side, then open public. Copy the prepared session's project folder from /public/module-04/ into your working folder at ~/Documents/netid/, then open the Set inside it.

Before you change anything, play it through once and listen the way a mixing engineer does on a first pass. Name the parts and their jobs: which one has the melody, which holds the rhythm, which fill in behind. Then listen for what the mix needs. Is one part so loud it covers the others? Is something buried too low to hear? Are two parts in the same place, so you can't hear either one clearly? Don't fix anything yet. On this pass, you're finding out what the mix has to solve.

make a mixing version

Up to now your work has been creation: choosing sounds, playing parts, arranging them into a piece. Creation decides what happens in the music. Mixing decides how the finished parts are heard, and it doesn't change what any part plays. It sets how loud each part is against the others (level), where each one is from left to right (pan), how near or far it feels (space, mostly from reverb), how parts share the frequency range so they don't mask each other (tone, from EQ), and how steady each part holds its level (dynamics, from compression).

A MIDI track holds notes and an instrument that turns those notes into sound as the piece plays. That sound changes the moment you edit a note or swap the instrument. Live can mix a MIDI track's output as it plays, but in this lab you bounce every MIDI track to audio before mixing, fixing its sound in place. Once every track is audio, Live's mixer works like the Toft from Module 3: one audio channel per part, with faders, sends, and a master.

Bouncing a track to audio is permanent. It replaces the notes and the instrument with a plain recording, and that track can no longer change what it plays. You mix on a separate saved copy. The original keeps your notes and instruments and stays open to edits. The mixing copy is the bounced, all-audio version you balance. If a mix decision shows that a part was written wrong, you reopen the original, fix it there, and bounce it again. Many producers keep the writing session and the mixing session as separate files.

save a separate version

Save the session as it is first: File then Save. Then make the mixing copy with File then Save As, named lastname-wk12-mix. Work in that copy from here on.

bounce the MIDI tracks to audio

Each MIDI track plays its instrument live as the piece runs. You turn that performance into a fixed recording in two steps: freeze, then flatten. Freezing renders the sound to a file; flattening makes that file the track's content for good.

What bouncing means

Bouncing means printing what a track plays into a fixed audio file. The word comes from tape: engineers bounced several recorded tracks down onto one to free up room on the reel. Here you bounce each MIDI track in place, turning the instrument's live output into audio in the same spot on the timeline as the MIDI. After a bounce, the track no longer plays an instrument in real time. It plays a recording of it.

Right-click a MIDI track's name and choose Freeze Track. Live plays the track's instrument and effects through once, records the result to an audio file, and from then on plays that file in place of the live processing. The track turns blue. Freeze is a tool for saving computer power, and it's fully reversible: unfreeze and the notes and instrument come back, ready to edit. Freezing alone changes nothing for good.

Flatten makes it permanent. With the frozen track selected, choose Flatten from the Edit menu. Live deletes the MIDI clips and the instrument and keeps the audio it just rendered, and the MIDI track becomes a plain audio track. Do this for every MIDI track. There's no unflatten. The original Set still has every instrument and note.

consolidate every track into one clip

Every track holds audio now, but many hold it as a row of short clips, the pieces the music was built from. Each clip has its own gain and fades, with gaps between them, and a gain change or fade on one clip applies to that clip only. Consolidating joins each track's clips into one. In the Arrangement timeline, select everything with Cmd-A and press Cmd-J. For each track, Live renders its clips and the gaps between them into one continuous audio file, and the track becomes a single unbroken clip. The new files are written to the project's Samples folder and stay with the Set.

This is the kind of session a mixing engineer receives: a stack of audio channels with nothing left to build.

From here on, every fader, pan, and device is a setting you can undo, and the audio files stay unchanged. You could open a fresh project on the same files tomorrow and build a second, different mix from them.

mix

Switch to the mixer with Tab, which opens Session view. Each track is now a vertical channel strip, the same shape as a channel on the Toft. The strip holds the controls you mix with: a fader for how loud the track is, a pan control for where it is from left to right, and send knobs that tap a copy of the track off to a shared effect. The Master strip on the right sums every channel into the one stereo output you'll export. Your music is still in the Arrangement, and pressing play here plays it. This view changes only the controls in front of you. Leave the empty clip slots alone. They're for launching clips, a way of working you meet later.

set levels

Set levels first, and give them real time before you reach for anything else. A mix that sounds wrong is often out of balance. Work with your ears rather than your eyes. One way to start: pull every fader down, bring up the main part to a comfortable level, then raise each other part only until you can hear it clearly. Lead parts go louder, and supporting parts go a little below them. As you go, watch the Master meter on the right and keep it out of the red, the point where the summed signal clips and distorts.

place sounds with pan

Pan moves a track left or right across the stereo field, the space between your two ears. Pan separates parts: two parts in the same spot blur together, and when you slide one left and the other right, the ear hears each on its own. Spread the parts out, a little one way, a little the other, and the mix sounds wider. Keep the lowest parts, the kick and the bass, in the center. Low frequencies are hard for the ear to locate, and they have the most energy in a mix. Centered, that energy splits evenly between the two speakers.

group related tracks

When a few tracks belong together, the drums for instance, you can handle them as one. Select them and press Cmd-G. Live wraps them in a Group track (a bus, in console terms) whose fader moves all of them at once, the way a subgroup worked on the console (the Toft labels its subgroups submasters). After you balance the pieces inside the group, one fader rides the whole kit up or down against the rest of the mix. A group also gives you one channel where you can treat the whole submix at once. Group what moves together.

shape with inserts

An insert is a device in a track's chain. It processes that one signal in series, in the order you stack the devices on it. Drag a device from the browser's Audio Effects onto a track, a group, or the master to add it there. Each dynamics tool goes at a different stage: a single track, a group, or the master.

On a single track, two tools shape one part. EQ Eight lifts or cuts chosen frequency ranges, the equalizer from Module 2. In a mix, it makes room: when two parts crowd the same range and mask each other, cut that range on the part that needs it less, and you can hear both. Compressor, the dynamics tool from Module 2, evens out a part that swings between loud and quiet and keeps its level steady in the balance. Put EQ before Compressor in the chain. The compressor then reacts to the sound you've already shaped.

On a group, the Glue Compressor does a gentler job across a whole submix. It's Ableton's version of the SSL bus compressor you met in the Module 3 studio, and it makes several tracks sound like one unit. A light touch on the drum group glues the kit. Use one Glue Compressor on the group, not one on every track inside it.

On the master, a Limiter goes last in the chain and catches the loudest peaks, setting a ceiling the signal can't cross. That ceiling keeps the exported file from clipping. Use one Limiter, on the Master strip, at the very end.

Add EQ and compression to a track only where you hear the need. Compression on every track usually means a level problem went unfixed. Live has more tone and dynamics devices than these, listed in the manual's Live Audio Effect Reference. For this mix, start with the four above.

add space with the sends

The other way to place an effect is a send. Where an insert processes one track alone, a send taps a copy of a track's signal and routes it to a separate return track, where one effect treats everything sent to it. Your Set already has two returns ready: return A holds a Reverb and return B holds a Delay, shared and waiting for tracks to feed them. A reverb on a return gives several parts one shared room, and the mix sounds like one space. One shared reverb also uses far less computer power than ten separate ones.

Every channel has a Send A and a Send B knob. Turn Send A up on a track and a copy of it feeds the reverb: the dry track stays exactly where it is, and a wet, roomy copy is added on top. Turn Send B up and that track picks up the delay instead. The send knob sets each track's wet amount into the shared effect. A little send on several tracks puts them in one space.

To change the reverb itself, click return A to select it and its Reverb opens in the device area along the bottom. Adjust it there, or swap it for a different one: drag Hybrid Reverb from the browser onto the return in place of the stock device. Hybrid Reverb is Live's larger reverb, two reverb engines running at once. It uses more computer power and makes richer, deeper spaces. Whatever you set applies to every track sending to A. Return B works the same way for its Delay, where Echo is the fuller alternative: a delay with two delay lines and its own filtering and modulation.

more devices

Utility, on every track

Utility is the small device your Lab 1 gain-staging diagram pointed to, and it goes on every track. Its Gain knob sets a track's level without moving the fader. The Width control narrows or widens the stereo image: spread a narrow pad wider, or pull in a part that's too wide. Bass Mono sums everything below a frequency you set to mono, in the center, while the rest of the track stays in stereo. A Mono button sums the whole track to one channel, a quick check of how the mix sounds on a phone speaker or a club system.

§ Utility Live 11 manual ↗

Auto Filter, a dedicated filter

Auto Filter is a filter on its own. Use it when you want to roll off a range and EQ Eight is more than you need. It has the filter types you know (low-pass, high-pass, band-pass) and a few more, and you set the cutoff and resonance by dragging in its display. As an insert it often goes at the end of a track's chain, after the dynamics. It can also sweep its own cutoff with an envelope or an LFO, which makes a moving, rhythmic filter. For a clean mix you'll mostly leave the cutoff fixed.

§ Auto Filter Live 11 manual ↗

Multiband Dynamics, and a de-esser

Multiband Dynamics splits a sound into three frequency bands, low, mid, and high, and compresses or expands each one on its own. Use it when one band of a sound is a problem: a boomy low end you want to tame, or a harsh top you want to bump down, while the rest stays as it is. Each band has an Above section that pulls down whatever gets too loud and a Below section that lifts whatever gets too quiet, set by their two thresholds.

Multiband Dynamics can work as a de-esser, which tames sibilance, the sharp ess and sh sounds in a voice. A de-esser is mostly a compressor that acts on one high band. To build one, put Multiband Dynamics on the voice, then turn off the Low and Mid bands with the buttons on the left, leaving only the High band on. Set the High band's crossover to start somewhere between five and eight kilohertz, where sibilance is in most voices, and solo that band to hear and tune the range. Then lower the High band's Above threshold until the meter dips only on the sibilance. Unsolo, and the sibilance is quieter while the rest of the voice stays as it was.

export the mix

Exporting renders the Master output, the sum of every channel, down to one stereo audio file. Open the dialog with File then Export Audio/Video, or Cmd-Shift-R, the same export as the last labs. Set it to 48 kHz, 32-bit, save it into your working folder, and name it lastname-wk12-mix.wav.
