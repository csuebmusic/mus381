Module 04 · Audio, sampling & mixing in Ableton

The DAW Environment

What a full DAW does, how audio and MIDI differ, and how we use each in this course.

You spent Modules 2 and 3 in Audacity, first editing sound and then recording your own. This module moves you into Ableton Live, the program the rest of the course is built around. Live does more than Audacity, and it works with a second kind of material alongside audio, called MIDI. Getting comfortable with that difference is what this first week is about.

1 · The DAW environment

A DAW, a digital audio workstation, is software for recording, editing, arranging, and mixing sound. You have already used one: Audacity. Ableton Live is a fuller version of the same idea. Where Audacity mostly handles a few tracks of recorded sound at a time, Live is built to run many tracks together, to play instruments you trigger from a keyboard, and to mix the whole thing through a built-in mixer. The rest of the course lives inside it.

Real-time, and nondestructive

Two ideas from Module 2 matter even more here.

The first is that Live works in real time. When you add a fade, change a volume, or put an effect on a track, Live does not rewrite the sound on the disk. It performs the change as the audio plays, every time you press play, computing it on the spot. Turn the effect off and the sound returns to what it was, because the change was never written down.

The second follows from the first. Editing in Live is nondestructive: your edits never alter the original sound files. When you cut, trim, or fade, Live changes how it reads a file, not the file itself. The recording on the disk stays whole. (In Audacity some edits were destructive, changing the file directly, which is why you kept careful copies. In Live you can relax that habit.) The payoff is freedom: you can try anything, because you cannot ruin your source.

MIDI: the data of music

Live works with two kinds of material. The first you know: audio, the recorded sound itself, the waveforms you edited in Audacity. The second is new: MIDI.

MIDI is not sound. It is data, editable information about music. A line of MIDI says which note, when it starts, how long it lasts, and how hard it was played. It carries the instructions for music while carrying no sound of its own. Think of a player-piano roll: held up to your ear it makes no sound, but fed through the piano, its holes tell the instrument what to play. MIDI is that roll, written as data.

Because MIDI is data, it is endlessly editable. Long after you play a passage you can move a note, change its pitch, make it louder, or shift its timing, the same way you would edit a line of text.

Two kinds of track

A DAW like Live keeps audio and MIDI side by side. A track is a lane that holds one kind of material: an audio track carries recorded sound, a MIDI track carries note data. They live in the same project and play together, so a piece can run recorded audio on some tracks and MIDI-driven sound on others. Live shows you both at once.

A MIDI track makes no sound on its own. Its notes drive an instrument, and the instrument makes the sound you hear.

How we use MIDI in this course

We use MIDI in one specific way: to trigger samples.

First, the word sample. You met it in Module 2, where it meant a single measurement of a sound wave, thousands taken every second. Here it means something different: a sample is a chunk of recorded audio, a sound you can load and play. Your Module 3 library is full of them. Same word, two meanings, and in this module a sample is the chunk of sound.

The instrument on a MIDI track will be one that plays your samples. A MIDI note tells a sample to sound: press a key and a sample plays; press a higher key and the same sample plays higher. That is the whole role MIDI has here. We are not using it to compose music note by note, writing melodies and harmonies into a grid. That is its own craft, and a rich one. If it draws you in, it lives in my advanced sound design courses and in applied composition and music production, where there is room to chase it.
