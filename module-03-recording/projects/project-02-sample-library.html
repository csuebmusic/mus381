Module 03 · Recording, sample prep & library building

Project 2: sample library

A curated, organized collection of your own recorded and prepped sounds, built across Module 3 and submitted as the midterm.

Across Module 3 you've been recording your own sounds and preparing them for use. Project 2 is the library those sounds add up to: a collection you record, clean, name, and organize into a structure you can find your way around. It's the recording half of the midterm (the terminology exam is the other half, on the same day), and it's the raw material you'll build with in Module 4. Leave Module 3 with a usable palette of your own.

what you turn in

  • Turn in at least 20 prepped sounds, with 20 to 25 as the target.
  • Use only your own recordings, made in the lab and on your phone. No downloaded or borrowed sounds.
  • Each sample is a mono WAV, 48 kHz, 24-bit (the Module 3 standard).
  • Every sample goes through the three-step pipeline from Lab 1: denoise, trim with short fades, normalize to -1 dBFS.
  • Every file is named [category]-[descriptor]-[variant].wav, lowercase, hyphens, no spaces (for example paper-crumble-slow.wav or metal-pan-strike-hard.wav).
  • Files are sorted into category folders, with a README.txt you write describing what's in the library and how it's laid out.
  • Work in Audacity.

what makes a strong library

A library is graded on how usable it is, not on how unusual the sounds are. A strong library shows five things.

  • You recorded cleanly: good levels with headroom, a low noise floor, and no clipping.
  • You prepped consistently. Every sample went through the same pipeline, with matched peak levels and clean starts and ends.
  • You named with meaning. The category, descriptor, and variant describe the sound, and a variant marks a real difference between sibling takes (slow vs fast, close vs far).
  • You organized for finding. Categories group like with like, and the README tells a stranger, or you in three months, what's here.
  • You curated a range: the library covers enough different sounds to be worth drawing from.

You don't need a sound from every category. A library built from paper, a kitchen, and a walk across campus can be excellent.

on organization

Use the structure you set up in Lab 1: a folder per category, the Audacity project kept in its own folder, and a README at the top.

sample-library/
  paper/
    paper-crumble-slow.wav
    paper-crumble-fast.wav
    paper-rip-slow.wav
    paper-rip-fast.wav
  metal/
    metal-pan-strike-soft.wav
    metal-pan-strike-hard.wav
    metal-wire-scrape-slow.wav
  water/
    water-drip-slow.wav
    water-pour-steady.wav
  audacity-projects/
    sample-library.aup3
  README.txt

Category names are yours to choose, as long as they group sounds the way you think about them. The README is short: list the categories, say roughly what's in each, and note anything a listener should know (a sample that's louder than the rest, a pair meant to be used together). Write it for someone who's never seen your library.

how to build it

The library grows across the whole module.

  • Start in Lab 1. Your first four paper sounds are the library's first entries, and you set up the folder and the naming convention that day.
  • Record between sessions, using the phone reference card. Take short walks and record a handful of sounds at a time.
  • Prep as you go: run new recordings through the pipeline soon after you make them, rather than leaving a backlog of raw files for the last week.
  • Upload every session. The master copy of the library is on the class server: download at the start, upload at the end, every time.

The library is due at the start of class on Wed Wk 9, before the terminology exam. Your instructor checks each library for location, structure, README, formats, naming, and count before the exam begins. Arrive with it uploaded and ready.

rubric

The sample library is graded out of 100 points across five dimensions, equally weighted at 20 points each. The library and the terminology exam together make up the midterm.

recording quality (20)

  • Levels recorded with headroom, peaks around -12 to -6 dBFS, no clipping
  • Low noise floor: clean captures, not buried in hiss or room rumble
  • The sound you meant to record is the sound that's there

sample prep (20)

  • Denoise applied cleanly, without the watery artifacts of an over-aggressive pass
  • Trimmed to the sound, with short fades and no clicks at the start or end
  • Normalized to a consistent peak ceiling (-1 dBFS)
  • Each file a mono WAV at 48 kHz, 24-bit

organization (20)

  • Sorted into category folders that group like with like
  • A README that describes the contents clearly enough for someone else to navigate
  • The Audacity project kept in audacity-projects/, not loose among the samples

naming (20)

  • Every file follows [category]-[descriptor]-[variant].wav
  • Lowercase, hyphens, no spaces or special characters
  • Descriptors and variants are meaningful: a variant marks a real difference between sibling takes

range & curation (20)

  • At least 20 prepped samples
  • Enough variety to be a usable palette rather than 20 versions of one sound
  • Evidence of choice: weak or duplicate takes left out instead of padded in

what isn't graded

  • How exotic the sounds are doesn't affect the grade.
  • Gear isn't graded. Phone recordings are expected and welcome, and the rubric looks at how well a sound is recorded, on whatever device.
  • Count beyond the minimum adds nothing to the grade. Don't pad.
  • Your instructor grades by the rubric, and personal taste isn't part of it.
