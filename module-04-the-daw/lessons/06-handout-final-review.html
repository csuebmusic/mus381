Module 04 · The DAW

final review

A study packet for the cumulative final. It gathers the Module 4 vocabulary (the DAW, the clip, MIDI as a trigger, the Ableton mixer) in one place, with the handful of earlier ideas the final still tests. Read each definition, then check that you can give it back in your own words and point to where it shows up in your own Ableton work.

how the exam works

The final is cumulative, and most of it covers Module 4. The Wk 9 midterm tested the vocabulary of the first half of the course. From earlier modules, the final re-tests three cross-course anchors: sample rate, clipping, and the bit-depth step at export. All three are in section 9.

Expect four kinds of thinking with the terms below: define a term in plain language, identify a part or a move from a description (which view you'd commit a final arrangement in, which instrument spreads one sample per pad), map or trace a path (the Ableton mixer onto the Module 3 console, a sample from your library to a finished export), and answer a short conceptual question (why nondestructive editing means you can't ruin a source, what a MIDI note does).

How to use this packet

Cover the definitions and read only the terms. For each one, say what it is out loud or write it down, then uncover and compare. The terms you can already give back are done. The ones you stumble on are your study list. The full explanations are in the Module 4 reading and handouts. For the earlier material the final still touches (sample rate, bit depth, clipping), the midterm review packet is the fuller reference.

1 · what a DAW is

From Module 4 · Into the DAW

Modules 2 and 3 used Audacity, an audio editor. Module 4 uses the course's first DAW, Ableton Live. The exam expects you to draw the line between the two.

DAW (digital audio workstation)
A full production environment: multitrack, nondestructive, with instruments, MIDI, and a built-in mixer.
Audio editor
A tool whose scope is recording and editing audio. A DAW adds instruments, MIDI, and a full mixer. Audacity is an audio editor; so is Adobe Audition.
Live Set
Ableton's word for a project, called a Set for short. It holds your tracks, clips, and settings, and saves as a .als file.
Track
A lane in the Set that holds clips. An audio track holds audio; a MIDI track holds MIDI notes and an instrument that turns them into sound.

2 · the two views

From Module 4 · Into the DAW

One Set, two ways to look at it. Both show the same tracks; they differ in what they're for.

Session view
The grid of clips, for trying ideas and looping. You launch clips and try combinations without committing to an order.
Arrangement view
The linear timeline, left to right, for committing to a structure. It resembles Audacity's timeline. Most early work in this module happens here. You commit a final arrangement in Arrangement view.

3 · the clip and nondestructive editing

From Module 4 · Into the DAW

Everything you learned about editing in Module 2 still applies, but what an edit touches has changed.

Clip
A reference to a file. It points at the audio on disk and has its own start, end, gain, fades, and warp settings. The clip isn't the file.
Nondestructive editing
Edits are stored on the clip, not in the file. Shortening a clip or adding a fade changes the clip's instructions; the file's samples stay exactly as recorded. You can't ruin your source, and you can change or undo any clip edit.
Destructive editing (the Audacity contrast)
In Module 2, an edit rewrote the samples in the file: a cut removed them, a fade changed them. That's destructive editing. Ableton leaves the file untouched and keeps the edits on the clip.

4 · warping

From Module 4 · Audio editing in Ableton

Warping is how Ableton fits a clip to your Set's tempo. The only decision you usually make is whether you want it on or off.

Warping
Ableton's time-stretching. A warped clip follows the Set's tempo instead of playing at the speed it was recorded.
Warp on / warp off
Warp on stretches a clip to match the Set's tempo: use it for loops and rhythmic material you want locked to the grid. Warp off plays the clip at its recorded speed: use it for one-shots and sounds where you want the original timing.
Warp markers
Pins that tie a moment in the audio to a moment in the bar. Ableton stretches the clip between them. You rarely need to set them by hand at this level.

5 · MIDI as a trigger

From Module 4 · Sampling in Ableton

MIDI is the new abstraction in this module. The model is small: a note is data, it triggers an instrument, and the instrument makes the sound.

MIDI note
Data, not sound. It has pitch (which note), timing (when), and velocity (how hard). On its own it makes no sound at all.
The trigger chain
A MIDI note triggers an instrument; the instrument makes the sound. The piano roll says play C3 now, medium-hard; the instrument turns that into audio.
MIDI track vs audio track
A MIDI track holds notes and an instrument that turns them into sound. An audio track holds recorded audio. Dropping a sample where you meant to draw a note, or the reverse, is the usual mix-up.

6 · the sampler instruments

From Module 4 · Sampling in Ableton

Two instruments turn your library from a folder of files into something you can play. Both take your own samples as their raw material.

Simpler
An instrument that plays one sample across the keyboard. A higher note plays the sample faster (higher), a lower note slower (lower). In Classic mode, each note has a volume envelope (attack, decay, sustain, release) that shapes it. One-Shot mode replaces the envelope with Fade In and Fade Out.
Drum Rack
A grid of pads, each pad holding one sample, each triggered by its own MIDI note. You use it to build a kit from your library sounds and play or sequence it.
Envelope (ADSR)
Attack, decay, sustain, release: the four stages that shape a note's level over time. The same envelope idea you met in Module 2 editing, now a control on the instrument.

7 · the Ableton mixer as the digital console

From Module 4 · Mixing in Ableton, with Module 3's console

The Ableton mixer has the layout of the Module 3 Toft console in software: channels, sends, groups, and a master. For each Ableton part, know the console part it maps to.

Track fader
Sets a track's level into the mix. The console's channel fader.
Pan
Places a track across the stereo field, left to right. The console's pan.
Send and return track
A send routes a copy of a track's signal to a return track, where a shared effect (reverb, delay) is loaded. One reverb can take signal from many tracks. On the console, these are the aux send and aux return.
Group track
Several tracks routed into one channel for combined level and processing. The console's subgroup, which the Toft labels a submaster.
Master track
The final stereo bus every track sums into, with the master fader on it. The console's master mix.

8 · inserts, sends, and the built-in devices

From Module 4 · Mixing in Ableton

The same effect types you met in Modules 2 and 3 are here as Live devices, in two roles. Where a device goes decides whether its order matters and whether other tracks can share it.

Insert effect
A device in a track's own chain, processing only that track. Order matters: the signal passes through each device in turn.
The insert chain order
On a track, gain comes first (Utility, on every track), then EQ (EQ Eight), then compression (Compressor), with Auto Filter often at the end. Multiband Dynamics goes on a single track when one band needs its own control, as in the de-esser. A group takes the Glue Compressor, and the master ends with a Limiter.
Send effect
A device on a return track, shared by any track that sends to it. The reverbs and delays go here (Hybrid Reverb, Reverb, Delay, Echo), and several tracks share one.
Insert vs send
Put an effect on the track itself (insert) when only that track needs it. Send to a return when several tracks share one effect. The same choice you met on the console as aux versus insert.

9 · concepts that apply across tools

From Module 4 · Adobe Audition, plus the cross-course anchors

These are properties of digital audio and audio production, and they apply in any tool. The next tool will look different and work the same way. Three of them are the earlier ideas the final still tests.

Sample rate
How many samples are taken per second, in Hz or kHz. The course standard is 48 kHz. (Cross-course anchor.)
Bit depth and the export step
You worked at 24-bit through Modules 2 and 3. You export the finished piece at 32-bit (48 kHz, 32-bit). The rate already matches your library, and importing resamples nothing. Only the bit depth steps up, and only at export. (Cross-course anchor.)
Clipping
When a signal goes past the digital ceiling (0 dBFS) and its peaks are flattened off. Clipping in a recording can't be undone in editing. In a mix, the summed signal can clip at the master, and a Limiter at the end of the master chain keeps the export under the ceiling. Leave headroom to avoid it. (Cross-course anchor.)
Transferable concepts
Sample rate, bit depth, gain staging, EQ, compression, fades, multitrack arrangement. Each of them appears in Audacity, Ableton, and Audition.
Adobe Audition
An audio editor in the Adobe suite, and this module's example of the same ideas in a different tool. Its Multitrack Editor has nondestructive editing and live effects, like a DAW. It lacks instruments and MIDI, and by this course's definition that makes it an audio editor.

10 · from library to finished piece

From Module 4 · The whole module

The deliverable for the second half of the course is the final piece, built in Ableton from your own recorded sound.

Your library as raw material
The sample library you built in Module 3 is the source for everything here. It's already 48 kHz, and it imports into a 48 kHz Set with nothing to convert.
The final project
Any kind of piece, 2 to 3 minutes, built in Ableton, demonstrating the semester's skills. It's drafted twice (Wk 14 and Wk 15) and uploaded as a finished WAV during finals.
Sourcing and credit
Every sound must be yours: your library plus anything new you record. The one exception is another student's recording, used with permission and credited in a credits file.

the thread that connects it all

A sound starts as pressure in the air. In Module 3 a microphone turned it into a mic-level signal, a cable carried it to the interface, a preamp raised it to line level, an ADC converted it to digital audio, and the software saved it as a WAV at 48 kHz, 24-bit. You prepped that recording into a sample and filed it in your library. In Module 4 you import that sample into a Set, where it becomes a clip that points at the file without changing it. You arrange it on the timeline, or load it into Simpler or a Drum Rack and trigger it with a MIDI note. You mix it through the same channel faders, aux sends, subgroups, and master you used on the Toft. Then you export the result at 48 kHz, 32-bit. Every term in this packet names a stage or a decision on that one path.

The console shows up twice

Module 3 taught what the console is: channels, faders, buses, aux sends, subgroups, the master mix. In Module 4 you mix with the same layout in software. Section 7 maps each part.

Nondestructive editing reframes Module 2

The editing moves from Audacity (cut, fade, gain) work the same way in Ableton, and on a clip you can undo them at any time. Section 3 has the clip-and-file model.
