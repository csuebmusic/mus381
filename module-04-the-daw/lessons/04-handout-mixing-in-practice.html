Mixing in Ableton

Commit a finished session to audio, then mix it.

This week you mix. Mixing means balancing a finished piece so every part sits in its place and the whole thing reads clearly. In Module 3 you met the console that does this, the Toft. Now you drive those same moves yourself, in Live's mixer. The session is waiting for you on the NAS: a longer piece with several instruments, some played from MIDI, some recorded as audio.

Today's gear

Take from the lab's gear storage: audio interface and headphones. Mixing is done with faders and devices, so the keyboard stays in the drawer today. Run through the start-of-session steps on the Session Routines card before continuing here.

What we're doing today

  1. Copy the session from the NAS and listen to it.
  2. Save a separate mixing version.
  3. Bounce the MIDI tracks to audio.
  4. Consolidate every track into one clip.
  5. Mix: levels, pan, a shared reverb, groups, EQ and compression.
  6. Export the mix and upload to the NAS.
Before you start

This lab has no videos, so the manual pages linked at each step are your reference. Read the step, do it, and open the manual when you want more. Keep the Session Routines card visible.

If you run short on time, set your levels and a little pan, export that, and upload. A rough balance handed in beats a detailed mix left on the desk.

Get the session

During the start-of-session download, copy the prepared session's project folder from the NAS into your working folder at ~/Documents/lastname/, then open the Set inside it.

Before you change anything, play it through once and listen the way a mixing engineer does on a first pass. Name the parts and their jobs: which one carries the melody, which holds the rhythm, which fill in behind. Then listen for what the mix needs. Is one part so loud it covers the others? Is something buried too low to hear? Do two parts sit in the same place so neither comes through? You are not fixing anything yet. You are finding out what the mix has to solve.

Make a mixing version

Up to now your work has been creation: choosing sounds, playing parts, arranging them into a piece. Creation decides what happens in the music. Mixing decides how the finished parts are heard, and it does not change what any part plays. It sets how loud each part is against the others (level), where each sits from left to right (pan), how near or far it feels (space, mostly from reverb), how parts share the frequency range so they do not mask each other (tone, from EQ), and how steady each part holds its level (dynamics, from compression). Those are the moves you make later in this lab, and every one of them acts on parts that are already written.

Mixing acts on audio, and a MIDI track is not audio yet. It holds notes and an instrument that turns those notes into sound as the piece plays, and that sound still changes the moment you edit a note or swap the instrument. You cannot set a fixed level or tone for something that can still move under you. So before mixing, you commit every MIDI track to audio, fixing its sound in place. Once every track is audio, they are all the same kind of thing, and the Live mixer becomes the Toft from Module 3: one audio channel per part, with faders, sends, and a master.

Committing a track to audio is permanent. It replaces the notes and the instrument with a plain recording, and that track can no longer change what it plays. That is why you mix on a separate saved copy. The original keeps your notes and instruments and stays open to edits. The mixing copy is the committed, all-audio version you balance. If a mix decision shows that a part was written wrong, you reopen the original, fix it there, and commit it again. Studios split the work the same way, keeping the session where a piece is written apart from the session where it is mixed.

Save a separate version

Save the session as it is first: File then Save. Then make the mixing copy with File then Save As, named lastname-wk12-mix so it sits beside the original and reads as the mix. Work in that copy from here on. Every step below changes the music for good, and the original stays untouched for the day you need to go back and rewrite something.

Bounce the MIDI tracks to audio

Each MIDI track plays its instrument live as the piece runs. You turn that performance into a fixed recording in two steps: freeze, then flatten. Freezing renders the sound to a file; flattening makes that file the track's content for good.

What bouncing means

Bouncing means printing what a track plays into a fixed audio file. The word comes from tape: engineers bounced several recorded tracks down onto one to free up room on the reel. Here you bounce each MIDI track in place, turning the instrument's live output into audio that sits right where the MIDI was. After a bounce, the track no longer plays an instrument in real time. It plays a recording of it.

Right-click a MIDI track's header and choose Freeze Track. Live plays the track's instrument and effects through once, records the result to an audio file, and from then on plays that file in place of the live processing. The track turns blue. Freeze exists to save computer power, and it is fully reversible: unfreeze and the notes and instrument come back, ready to edit. On its own, freeze commits nothing.

Flatten makes it permanent. With the frozen track selected, choose Flatten from the Edit menu. Live throws out the MIDI clips and the instrument and keeps the audio it just rendered, so the MIDI track turns into a plain audio track. Do this for every MIDI track. There is no unflatten, which is the whole reason you are in the mixing copy: the original still holds every instrument and note.

Consolidate every track into one clip

Every track holds audio now, but many hold it as a row of short clips, the pieces the music was built from. A row of fragments is awkward to mix: each clip carries its own gain and fades, gaps fall between them, and a change you make on one clip has to be repeated on the next. Consolidating clears that up. Select everything with Cmd-A and press Cmd-J. For each track, Live renders its clips and the gaps between them into one continuous audio file, so the track becomes a single unbroken clip you can treat as one thing. The new files land in the project's Samples folder and travel with the Set.

Every track is now one unbroken clip of audio. This is the session a mixing engineer sits down to: a stack of audio channels with nothing left to build, ready to balance.

Mix

Switch to the mixer with Tab, which opens Session view. Each track now stands as a vertical channel strip, the same shape as a channel on the Toft. The strip carries the controls you mix with: a fader for how loud the track is, a pan control for where it sits left to right, and send knobs that tap a copy of the track off to a shared effect. The Master strip on the right sums every channel into the one stereo output you will export. Your music still lives in the Arrangement, so press play to hear it; this view only changes the controls in front of you. Leave the empty clip slots alone, they belong to a way of working you meet later.

Set levels

Level is the first move and the one that fixes the most, so give it real time before reaching for anything else. Most mixes that sound wrong are not missing an effect, they are out of balance. Work with your ears rather than your eyes. One reliable way in: pull every fader down, bring up the part that carries the music to a comfortable level, then raise each other part only until you can hear it doing its job. Lead parts sit forward, supporting parts sit under them. As you go, watch the Master meter on the right and keep it out of the red, the point where the summed signal clips and distorts.

Place sounds with pan

Pan moves a track left or right across the stereo field, the space between your two ears. Its real use is separation: two parts in the same spot fight for the same place and blur together, but slide one left and the other right and the ear hears each on its own. Spread the parts out, a little one way, a little the other, and the mix opens up. Keep the lowest parts, the kick and the bass, in the center. Low frequencies are hard for the ear to locate and they carry the most energy, so centering them holds that energy even across both speakers.

Add reverb on a send

This is the spot in mixing routing that trips people up, so take it slowly. An effect can go in two places. An insert sits in one track's chain and processes that track alone, which is how you will use EQ and compression below. A send works differently: it taps a copy of a track's signal and routes it to a separate return track, where a single effect processes everything sent to it. Reverb belongs on a send. You want one room that several parts share, so the mix sounds like one space instead of a stack of separate rooms, and one reverb costs the computer far less than ten.

From the Create menu, choose Insert Return Track, and drag a Reverb from the browser's Audio Effects onto it. Every channel now shows a Send knob feeding that return. Turn a track's send up and more of that track goes into the reverb: the dry track stays exactly where it is, and the wet copy is added on top. The send knob is that track's wet amount into the one shared room.

Group related tracks

Once a set of tracks belongs together, the drums for instance, you can handle them as one. Select them and press Cmd-G. Live wraps them in a Group track whose fader moves all of them at once, the way a subgroup worked on the console. After you balance the pieces inside the group, one fader rides the whole kit up or down against the rest of the mix. Grouping also turns a wall of single tracks into a few blocks, drums, then bass, then the rest, which are far easier to balance against each other. Group what moves together.

Shape with EQ and compression

Level and pan place a part. Two more tools shape it, and both are inserts that sit in the one track's chain. EQ Eight lifts or cuts chosen frequency ranges, the equalizer from Module 2. Its main job in a mix is room: when two parts crowd the same range and mask each other, cut that range on the one that needs it less and both come through. Compressor evens out a part that swings between loud and quiet, the dynamics tool from Module 2, so it holds a steady place in the balance instead of jumping forward and falling back. Order matters, because each device feeds the next: put EQ before Compressor so the compressor reacts to the sound you have already shaped. Reach for these only where a part asks for one. An effect on every track is usually a level problem you skipped.

Export the mix

Exporting renders the Master output, the sum of every channel, down to one stereo audio file. Open the dialog with File then Export Audio/Video, or Cmd-Shift-R, the same export as the last labs. Set it to 48 kHz, 32-bit, save it into your working folder, and name it lastname-wk12-mix.wav.

End of session

What to upload today

  1. In Live, run File → Collect All and Save. Freezing and consolidating wrote new audio into the project's Samples folder, and this gathers all of it into the Set's Project folder so nothing goes missing.
  2. During the NAS-upload phase of the routine, upload your lastname/ working folder to [path]/students/lastname/, choosing Replace when prompted. The lastname-wk12-mix Project folder and your exported WAV go up with it.
  3. Verify the lastname-wk12-mix Project folder and lastname-wk12-mix.wav are on the NAS.

Then continue with the rest of the card's end-of-session routine: eject the NAS, sign out of browser accounts, quit all apps, knobs back to zero, unplug everything, stow the gear back in the lab's gear storage, chair in.
