compression & limiting in Audacity

From the tool's sliders to Audacity's menus.

In the compressor tool you moved a compressor's parameters and heard what each one does. Audacity's Compressor has the same parameters plus two more, and the recipes below give you starting settings for compressing and limiting sounds for Project 1. Compression and limiting are the last two effects you'll apply before exporting your final piece. Read this once now, and once again with Audacity open before Wednesday's work session.

Today's gear

Take from the lab's gear storage: audio interface, headphones. Plug in and run through the start-of-session steps on the Session Routines card before continuing here.

the two effects, and where to find them

Audacity 3.6 groups its dynamics processors under Effect → Volume and Compression. This module uses two of them: Compressor and Limiter. Both effects use the same processing engine, and their windows look almost identical.

You'll also see Legacy Compressor and Legacy Limiter further down the same submenu. These are the older versions that shipped with Audacity 3.5 and earlier, with their controls laid out differently. Use the non-legacy Compressor and Limiter for this course.

1 · the Compressor

Open a track with a clear difference between its loud and quiet parts (a recorded vocal line or a varied percussion loop, for example) and go to Effect → Volume and Compression → Compressor.

The window has three regions. The big graph at the top is the compression curve, the same thing as the transfer curve in the compressor tool: it shows input level along one axis and output level along the other, and it bends at the threshold to show how the compressor reshapes the signal. The sliders in the middle (Threshold, Make-up gain, Knee width, Ratio) set the shape of that curve. The sliders at the bottom (Lookahead, Attack, Release) set the timing: how fast the compressor moves into that shape and lets go of it. Audacity calls these timing controls the smoothing parameters.

the controls

Threshold is the level above which the compressor starts to engage, as in the tool. It's set in dB: 0 dB is the digital ceiling, and lower numbers are quieter.

Ratio works the same way it does in the tool. It sets how much the compressor reduces the signal above the threshold: a 4:1 ratio means that for every 4 dB the input goes above threshold, the output rises by only 1 dB.

Make-up gain is the boost applied to the whole signal after compression (the tool has it too). Compression lowers the loud parts, which makes the file quieter overall, and make-up gain restores the level. Use it when you want the compressed result to sound as loud as the source, or louder. Set it to 0 when you only want to tame peaks and keep the overall level where it was.

Knee width is one of the two controls the compressor tool doesn't have (the tool's knee is fixed at 2 dB). It sets how abruptly the compressor engages at the threshold. At a knee width of 0 dB (a "hard knee"), the compressor does nothing below the threshold and applies the full ratio immediately above it. The transition is a sharp corner. At a wider knee (say, 6 or 12 dB), the compressor starts engaging gradually a few dB before the threshold and reaches the full ratio a few dB after it, and the corner becomes a rounded curve. A wider knee sounds gentler and is harder to hear. A narrower knee is more obvious. For most musical work, a knee width between 3 and 6 dB sounds natural. For aggressive, limiting-style compression on transients, use 0 dB (a hard knee).

Attack is how fast the compressor engages once the signal crosses the threshold, as in the tool. A short attack catches transients (kick hits, snare cracks, consonants) and rounds them off. A longer attack lets transients pass through and compresses only the body of each hit.

Release is how fast the compressor lets go after the signal drops below the threshold. A short release recovers between events and can produce audible "pumping" on rhythmic material: the level ducks and recovers in time with the loud parts. A long release holds gain reduction across several events for a smoother overall feel.

Lookahead is the second control the compressor tool doesn't have. It lets the compressor read a few milliseconds ahead in the signal and start engaging before a loud peak, instead of reacting to the peak after the fact. Lookahead and attack both let the compressor catch a transient cleanly without smashing it. With attack alone, a small "overshoot" gets through (the start of the peak passes before the compressor catches up). Lookahead anticipates the peak and prevents the overshoot, and in exchange it adds a small delay (latency). Audacity compensates for this latency automatically. Start with 1 to 5 ms of lookahead for catching transients; longer values are for creative effects.

what the compression curve graph shows

The graph updates live as you move Threshold, Ratio, Make-up gain, and Knee width. Attack, Release, and Lookahead act over time, and the graph doesn't show them. Its scales are on different sides from the compressor tool's: input runs along the top axis rather than the bottom, and output along the right axis rather than the left.

realtime use, and the live graph

Audacity lets you apply the compressor in two ways. The first is to open the effect, set the parameters, and click Apply, which renders the change into the audio. The second is realtime use: the compressor is loaded onto a track's effects stack and processes the signal during playback without rendering anything. In realtime use, the graph at the top of the window adds live traces of the input level, the output level, and the gain reduction the compressor is applying. Dial in your settings in realtime use, while you listen.

A note on knee width

Changes in knee width make a small difference that's hard to hear. Set it to 3 or 6 dB for general work (the recipes below use 0 dB where they call for a hard knee) and spend your listening time on the other controls. Knee width makes the most difference when the threshold is near the average level of the signal, where the compressor engages and lets go many times per second. It makes less difference when the threshold is well above the average level, as in most compression for managing loudness. If a setting sounds harsh or obvious, try widening the knee. If you can't hear the compression and you want to, try narrowing it.

starting points for the compressor

Audacity's factory presets, under the Presets & settings button, let you hear a range of settings quickly. The voice presets are for spoken word; Beefy Master and Deep Dive Master are for a finished mix. Try a few and look at the settings each one uses. To start from scratch on Project 1 material, use the recipes below and adjust by ear.

Recipe · Taming a sound that's too jumpy in level

Use this when one sound in your bank has loud peaks that stick out of the mix while its average level is too quiet. With the peaks brought down, you can raise the whole sound to a useful working level without the peaks clipping.

Threshold−18 dB
Ratio4:1
Knee width3 dB
Attack10 ms
Release120 ms
Lookahead2 ms
Make-up gain0 dB to start; bring up if needed after

Play the sound. The peaks should come down by a few dB. If they don't come down enough, lower the threshold (toward −24 dB); if they come down too much, raise it (toward −12 dB). Once the peaks are tamed, normalize the result to a −1 dB peak as a separate step, or use make-up gain to get roughly the same result.

Recipe · Adding punch to a percussive sound

Use this when a kick or snare sample has the right energy but feels flat in the mix. A slow attack lets the transient pass through while the body of the hit gets compressed, which makes the transient feel comparatively bigger.

Threshold−24 dB
Ratio6:1
Knee width0 dB (hard knee)
Attack30 ms (slow)
Release80 ms
Lookahead0 ms (don't let it anticipate)
Make-up gain3 to 6 dB

Keep the attack slow. Keep Lookahead at 0: any lookahead starts compressing before the strike and flattens the transient.

2 · the Limiter

Open Effect → Volume and Compression → Limiter.

A limiter is a compressor with no Ratio control and no Attack control. Its ratio is effectively infinite: nothing crosses the ceiling. In place of attack, it uses Lookahead to anticipate peaks. Every other control works as it does in the Compressor, except that make-up gain becomes Make-up target.

the controls

Threshold is the level above which limiting engages, as in the Compressor. With an infinite ratio, the limiter holds anything above the threshold down to the threshold level.

Make-up target is the limiter's version of make-up gain. In the Compressor, make-up gain means "add this many dB to the output after compression." In the Limiter, the make-up target is the maximum output level: "after limiting, the loudest point should peak at this dB value." Setting Make-up target to −1 dB means "after limiting, my peaks will reach −1 dB and go no higher."

Knee width, Lookahead, and Release work the same way they do in the Compressor. With no Attack control, the limiter relies on lookahead to catch every peak before it crosses the threshold. Use 5 to 10 ms on a final-mix limiter, enough to detect most musical peaks before they arrive.

limiting as the final step

Put the limiter at the very end of your signal chain. By then you've compressed individual sounds, mixed them together, and set your levels, and the limiter raises the overall loudness without letting anything clip. Run it on your full mix: set the threshold a few dB below 0, set Make-up target to −1 dB, set the smoothing parameters as in the recipe below, and listen.

The more aggressively you compress and limit, the less dynamic range your final mix will have (the loud parts and quiet parts will be closer in level). Sometimes that's what you want, as in a dense, high-energy mix. Sometimes it isn't, as in a piece with wide swings between loud and quiet. Listen for it.

starting points for the limiter

Recipe · Final-mix limiter for Project 1

Apply this to your finished mix as the last effect before exporting. It brings the overall level up to a useful loudness and keeps every peak below 0 dB.

Threshold−6 dB
Make-up target−1 dB
Knee width3 dB
Lookahead5 ms
Release100 ms

Watch the gain reduction on the graph in realtime use. If the limiter is taking more than about 4 to 6 dB off the peaks, the mix itself is too loud: pull individual tracks down instead of pushing the limiter harder. Use the limiter for the last few dB.

Recipe · Safety limiter for an individual sample

Use this when a sample has the occasional sharp peak that you want to catch without changing the rest of the signal's character. Where the final-mix limiter raises the overall level, this one keeps a single problematic hit from distorting.

Threshold−3 dB
Make-up target−1 dB
Knee width0 dB (hard)
Lookahead3 ms
Release50 ms

Most of the signal stays below the threshold and isn't limited, though the whole sound comes up by the 2 dB between Threshold and Make-up target. Only the occasional peak gets limited.

3 · a workflow for Project 1

Finish your piece in this order.

First, prepare individual sounds. For each sample in your bank, decide whether it needs compression. A sample that's already well-behaved (consistent level, no big peaks) probably doesn't. A sample with wild dynamics, an unwanted transient, or a flat middle that you'd like to sound punchier almost certainly does. Apply the compressor to that sample, save the processed result, and use that version in your mix. Leave the well-behaved samples alone.

Second, set levels and mix: track volume, panning, and balance, the work you did in last Wednesday's lab. Leave compression off at this stage in most cases. A compressor changes levels as the sound plays, and that makes it harder to judge which sounds should be louder than which.

Third, apply a limiter to the final mix. Once the mix sounds right, save a copy of the project, select all its tracks, and choose Tracks → Mix → Mix and Render to combine them into one track. Apply the limiter to that track as the very last effect, starting from the recipe above. Export the final WAV at this module's standard format: 48 kHz, 24-bit.

Fourth, listen on different systems. What sounds good on headphones may sound different on laptop speakers or in a car. Limiting in particular can sound very different on a small speaker, where the reduced dynamic range can sound "loud and exciting" or "fatiguing," depending on the material. If you can, listen on at least two playback systems before deciding the final mix is done.

When the limiter sounds wrong

If your limited mix sounds "smaller" than the unlimited one, you've probably pushed the threshold too low. Pull it back up. If the limited mix sounds distorted or harsh, increase the lookahead and lengthen the release. If you can hear the whole mix pumping, the threshold is too low or the release is too short. With a well-set limiter, you barely hear the limiting: the mix sounds like itself, only louder.
