mixing in Audacity

Three new tools: track levels, stereo pan, and EQ. Then back to Project 1.

The basics of mixing are levels (how loud each track is), panning (where each track appears in the stereo field), and EQ (which frequencies get emphasized or reduced in a sound). Levels and panning are simple sliders on each track. EQ opens in a dialog and changes the audio itself.

destructive vs. non-destructive

A non-destructive tool changes how the audio plays back without changing the audio data itself. Move a slider, hear a difference; move it back, the original is exactly there. The underlying file isn't touched. Most things in modern DAWs (Ableton, Logic, Pro Tools) are non-destructive by default: every EQ, compressor, and reverb is a plugin on the track and can be reshaped at any time, even months later.

A destructive tool changes the audio data permanently. Once you apply it, the samples on disk are different. Undo can revert it during the current session, but once you save and close the project, the change is permanent.

Audacity is mostly an audio editor, and most things in Audacity are destructive. The Effect menu (every effect: fades, reverse, time-stretch, EQ, compression, all of them) modifies your audio in place. Destructive editing comes from tape: cut a section out or splice in a different one, and the tape is physically different. Audacity works the same way.

The two non-destructive mixing controls in Audacity are the track gain slider and the pan slider on the Track Control Panel. They're applied at playback, and moving them never changes the underlying audio. Audacity also has a Realtime Effects feature (the small Effects button on each track that lets you stack non-destructive plugins), but this course doesn't use it. The non-destructive plugin workflow comes in Module 4, in Ableton.

the three mixing tools

Two of the tools are sliders on each track's Track Control Panel, on the left side of the track:

1 · track levels

The volume of one track relative to the others. Mixers usually balance levels first. If a track is too loud, it overwhelms everything else; too quiet, and you don't hear it at all.

Where in Audacity
Each track has a small horizontal slider in its Track Control Panel on the left side. Look for a slider with a − on one end and a + on the other. Drag it left to make the track quieter, right to make it louder. The slider is called Gain (or sometimes Volume).

gain staging and balance

Set levels in two passes:

  1. First, make sure nothing peaks. No track should ever hit 0 dB on the meters. When a signal hits 0, the waveform gets clipped flat at the top, and the audio distorts. Once a track has clipped, the only fix is to lower its gain and play it back again. Keep peaks comfortably below 0, with the loudest moments peaking a little below −6 dB. The gap between your loudest peak and 0 is called headroom. This first pass is called gain staging.
  2. Then balance the tracks against each other. Once nothing is clipping, ask whether each element is at the level you want relative to the others. The "main" sound of a moment should be loudest, with supporting sounds quieter. As your piece moves through different moments, the balance can shift: the foreground in section A might step back in section B.

In the healthy mix on the left, every tick is well below −6 dB, in the green zone, and the four tracks are close to each other in level. In the problem mix on the right, T1 has hit clip and is distorting (its bar fills the green, orange, and red zones because its peak reached all the way to 0); T4 peaks in the orange hot zone; T2 is comfortably in the green; T3 is also in the green but so quiet (−28 dB) that it'll be hard to hear in the mix.

You don't have a single big meter for each track in Audacity (that's a feature of more elaborate DAWs), but you do have the playback meter at the top of the Audacity window, which shows the level of the whole mix. As your piece plays, watch that meter. If it ever turns red at the right edge, something is clipping. Lower the gain on whichever track is the culprit (often the loudest element), play again, check.

Exercise (in your Project 1 file)

Two passes through your piece. First, solo each track and check its levels on its own. Each track's Track Control Panel has a Solo button (next to Mute). For each track in turn: click Solo on, press play, watch the playback meter at the top of the Audacity window, and check whether anywhere in the track the signal reaches the orange or red zone or is too quiet to hear. Adjust that track's gain slider until peaks stay below −6. Click Solo off and move to the next track. This is the gain-staging pass.

Second, un-solo everything and play the whole piece. Now the question is balance: with all tracks playing together, can you hear every element at the relative loudness you want? Which moments push the meter close to red? Which tracks disappear when other things are playing? Move the gain sliders to balance them in context, in small adjustments (3 to 6 dB at a time). Keep adjusting until: (1) nothing peaks anywhere in the piece, and (2) every element you intend to be heard can be heard.

The solo habit

Solo a track whenever you adjust its level, EQ, or anything else. In the EQ exercise, the Filter Curve EQ Preview button plays the selected audio on its own.

Listen for: a 3 to 6 dB change is usually enough to bring a track forward or push it back. If you find yourself making 15 dB changes, something else is probably wrong (the source recording may be too loud, or two tracks have energy in the same frequency range, an EQ problem covered below).

Playback volume

The slider next to the playback meter at the top of the Audacity window sets the playback volume: how loud the project plays on your headphones. It doesn't change the project's audio or the exported file. Use it when the tracks are well balanced but the whole mix is too loud or too quiet to listen to comfortably. In Module 4, Ableton's master track can hold effects that shape the whole mix, such as a limiter.

2 · stereo pan

The position of a track in the stereo field, from full left to full right. Pan gives a mix space and width: instead of every sound stacked on top of each other in the center, sounds occupy different points across the stereo field, the way instruments occupy different points on a stage.

Where in Audacity
Just below the gain slider in each track's Track Control Panel is a second slider with L on one end and R on the other. This is the Pan slider. Drag it left to push the track toward the left speaker, right to push it toward the right. Centered (the default) means equal in both speakers.

how you hear position

Humans localize sound using two cues: interaural time difference (sound from the left arrives at the left ear a fraction of a millisecond before the right) and interaural intensity difference (sound from the left is slightly louder in the left ear because the head shadows the right). Together, these tiny differences let your brain place a sound somewhere in space.

When the same signal goes equally to both speakers (or both sides of a pair of headphones), there's no time or intensity difference between your ears, and the brain reads "this sound is straight ahead, between the speakers." That's a centered sound. When you pan to one side, you change the proportion sent to each speaker. That changes the intensity difference at your ears and moves the sound across the perceived space. Panning creates stereo width this way.

the stereo field

If you imagine looking down at a stage from above, with two speakers in front of you and you sitting in the audience, the space between and around the speakers is the stereo field. Mixers tend to place different kinds of sounds in different parts of it:

common pan positions

Pop and electronic mixers follow conventions about where different kinds of sounds go. They're conventions, and you can depart from them:

  • Low-frequency content (anything below roughly 150 Hz) goes in the center. Your ears localize low frequencies poorly, and panning bass to one side adds no stereo width and puts more low end in one speaker than the other. Mixers also center the focal point of a moment: a lead vocal, the kick drum in a beat, the sustained drone under a section. Centering makes a sound feel solid and present.
  • A slight pan left or right (around 20 to 40 percent) suits supporting elements that need their own space without disappearing into the background: hi-hats, supporting percussion, secondary voices, complementary textures.
  • A wider pan (around 50 to 70 percent) suits pads, ambient layers, and textural material that fills space without drawing attention. These sounds are already diffuse in character and can often be panned generously without sounding unbalanced.
  • A hard pan left or right (90 to 100 percent) suits special effects, deliberate spatial gestures, and ping-pong-style alternations between channels. Hard pans call attention to themselves. Use them sparingly.

In your Project 1 piece, the most foundational or lowest-pitched element of a moment usually goes in the center, and supporting and textural material can spread out. You don't have a kick drum and bass guitar to organize, but you do have your own foundation and supporting roles, even in a musique concrète piece.

Exercise (in your Project 1 file)

Put on headphones (the stereo effect is most obvious there). For each of your tracks, decide what role it plays: is it a foundational element, a supporting one, or a textural one? Then pan accordingly. Try one track at a slight L (around 25%), another at slight R (around 25%), and leave the most foundational one in the center. Press play and listen. Where does each sound seem to come from?

Listen for: the piece now feels wider. Sounds occupy different positions in your headphones rather than all coming from the center. A small pan (20 to 40 percent) sounds like a position in a room; a hard pan sounds like a split between channels. If something with a lot of low-frequency content suddenly feels lopsided when you pan it, trust that feeling: bring it back to center.

3 · EQ

EQ, short for equalization, lets you boost or cut specific frequency ranges in a sound. It shapes the timbre of a sound, its color, which you met in the Digital Audio Explorer: the same pitch played on a flute and a violin has different timbre because the harmonics above the fundamental are distributed differently. Another name for that distribution of frequency content is the spectrum.

EQ lets you reshape the spectrum: maybe a recording has too much rumble in the low end and you want to reduce it, or a voice sounds dull and you want to brighten the high end. EQ is easy to overuse. In Audacity, EQ is destructive, like the rest of the Effect menu.

Where in Audacity
Effect → EQ and Filters → Filter Curve EQ…

The dialog opens with a graph like this one:

You draw a curve by clicking on the graph to add control points and dragging them up or down. The curve shows what the EQ will do to each frequency range, exactly as drawn. Where the curve is at 0 dB, that frequency passes through unchanged. Where it bows up, that frequency gets boosted; where it bows down, that frequency gets cut.

Exercise (in your scratch project)

Switch to your scratch project window (lastname-techniques-scratch.aup3). Make sure the scratch window is the active one before you continue.

  1. Import a sound from the bank. Pick something with both low and high frequency content; a voice, a field recording, or a sustained instrument from sustain-long/ all work well. Avoid pure tones for this exercise.
  2. Select the entire sound (Cmd + A), or click and drag to select a chunk of the audio file.
  3. Open Effect → EQ and Filters → Filter Curve EQ… The dialog appears, with a flat horizontal line at 0 dB across the graph.
  4. Click somewhere on the graph around 200 Hz (the lower-left area) and drag down a few dB. You've created a control point that cuts that frequency range.
  5. Click somewhere around 4000 Hz (right of center) and drag up a few dB. You've created a second control point that boosts that range. The curve now bends down on the left and up on the right.
  6. Click the Preview button. Audacity plays the selected audio with the EQ applied, but doesn't modify it. Preview plays only the selected audio, with no other tracks, while you audition the curve. Listen carefully. The sound should be brighter in the upper-mids and thinner in the lower-mids than the original.
  7. Don't like it? Adjust the control points (drag them, or drag them off the graph to delete) and Preview again. Try a few different shapes. Keep previewing until you find a curve you might want to commit to. (Or until you've decided EQ doesn't help this sound, which is also a valid conclusion.)
  8. When you've heard a curve you like, then click Apply. The dialog closes and the EQ is now permanently part of the audio. Listen one more time without the dialog open.
  9. Now press Cmd + Z to undo. The audio reverts to its original state. Reapply with a different curve if you want.

Listen for: every EQ change reshapes the character of the sound. Cuts (curves that go below 0) tend to sound natural; boosts (curves that go above 0) tend to be more obvious and easier to overdo. A small change at the right frequency often changes the sound more than a large change at the wrong one.

Flatten and Invert

Flatten resets the curve to a flat line at 0 dB. Use this if your curve has gotten messy and you want to start over without closing the dialog. Invert flips the current curve upside down; what you were boosting becomes a cut, and vice versa. Use it to hear "the opposite" of the curve you just drew.
