peer listening: sample libraries

Browse and listen to your classmates' midterm libraries, and write about a few that gave you ideas.

After Wednesday's midterm submission, everyone's sample library is gathered in one place. Browse them, listen through a handful, and write briefly about three or four that gave you ideas. A library is a different thing from a finished piece: it's a set of choices about what was worth recording and keeping, a palette someone built. You're listening as a fellow builder, scouting what other people heard and how you might use it. The response is due before class on Mon Wk 10, the first day of Module 4, and we'll discuss the libraries then. Come prepared.

how to browse

The libraries are gathered at /public/mus-381-fall-2026/project-02-libraries/ on the class server, with one folder per classmate. Connect with FileZilla, click the / at the top of the directory tree on the right side, then open public and navigate down. You can reach the server from inside the lab only. Download what you want to hear there, and put a copy on a USB drive or your own cloud storage if you want to listen elsewhere. Start with a classmate's README.txt: it tells you how they organized things and what's in each category. Then listen through their sounds, category by category.

There are around 25 libraries, and you don't need to hear every sample of every one. Skim the whole set, then pick three or four libraries to go deeper on. The samples are short, a few seconds each, and a single library is quick to get through. Use headphones if you can.

Don't multitask while you browse.

what to write

Pick three or four libraries that gave you ideas, for whatever reason: a sound you wish you'd recorded, a category you wouldn't have thought of, an especially clean recording, a smart piece of organization, or a collection with a strong character. For each one:

  1. Say whose library it is (last name or folder).
  2. Name one specific thing you noticed, such as a sound that caught your ear, a category, an especially clean recording, a naming or organization choice, or the overall range. For example: "the kitchen category had three knife-on-board takes that each sounded usefully different."
  3. Name one specific file you'd borrow and say how you'd use it in a piece: loop it, pitch it down, layer it under something, turn it into a beat, stretch it into a drone. Module 4 opens with this question.

Write about 50 to 80 words per library, and keep each one specific.

tone

Be generous and curious. If a library didn't interest you, write about another one (there's no requirement to critique). The libraries you choose to write about are a kind of feedback too.

Bring your ideas for using classmates' sounds to the Mon Wk 10 discussion.

What you write about a classmate's library may end up in front of them. Write it the way you'd want to read feedback on your own library.
