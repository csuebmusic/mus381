Adobe Audition

You spent this term in two programs: Audacity, where an edit changed the file, and Ableton, where an edit changed a clip and left the file alone. Adobe Audition does both. It's built for shaping recorded sound rather than creating music. Podcasters and video editors use it, and it has no instruments. Today you run the editing moves you already know in Audition and see how many of them work the same way.

Today's gear

Take from the lab's gear storage: audio interface, headphones. Plug in and run through the start-of-session steps on the Session Routines card before continuing here.

Nothing today is graded or submitted. Experiment freely. You still run the end-of-session routine and upload your working folder at the end, the same as every session.

what we're doing today

  1. Open Audition's two editors, the two working styles you already used.
  2. Start a session and bring a few of your library sounds into it.
  3. In the Waveform Editor, edit one file: select, delete, fade.
  4. Clean a noisy recording from the class server: capture a noise print, then reduce.
  5. Try the Spectral Frequency Display, a kind of editing new to this course.
  6. In the Multitrack Editor, build a small mix: levels, pan, and one effect you recognize.
  7. Export the mix, then run the end-of-session routine.

two editors, two working styles you already have

Audition opens into one of two editors. The Waveform Editor works on a single audio file and edits it destructively: a cut changes the file, exactly like Audacity in Modules 2 and 3. The Multitrack Editor stacks clips on a timeline and edits them nondestructively: the edits are instructions layered on the clip, and the source files stay untouched, exactly like Ableton.

Three guides cover the same ground at different depths. Use whichever fits how you like to learn. Adobe's guide is the official documentation, the walkthrough is a current step-by-step, and John Roach's guide is a printable reference you can keep open beside you.

start a session and bring in your sounds

Make a multitrack session with File → New → Multitrack Session. Set the Sample Rate to 48000, the same rate you've worked at all term, and set Bit Depth to 32-bit. Name the session and save it in its own folder inside your working folder at ~/Documents/netid/. Audition saves the session as a .sesx file, which points at your audio.

If you've already disconnected, open FileZilla and connect again from the Quickconnect bar. Download a handful of sounds from sample-library/ in your own folder on the server into your session's folder. Bring them into the session with File → Import → File, or drag them onto the Files panel. The Files panel holds the media for this session, like the browser in Ableton.

edit a single file in the Waveform Editor

Double-click a sound in the Files panel. It opens in the Waveform Editor, the destructive side, where edits change the file itself. You're working from the copies in your session's folder, not your library originals, and it's safe to cut into them.

Pick the Time Selection tool (press T), then click and drag across a part you want gone, a long gap or a stray noise. Press Delete. In the Waveform Editor the file closes up around the cut, the same as deleting a selection in Audacity.

Smooth the start and end so they don't click. In the top-left corner of the waveform, drag the fade handle inward to make a fade-in, and drag the handle in the top-right corner for a fade-out. It's the same fade habit from Audacity: a hard edit boundary pops unless you ramp it.

clean up a noisy recording

You already ran this move in Module 3 when you prepped samples. You show the software a slice of pure background noise, then ask it to subtract that noise from the whole file.

In FileZilla, click the / at the top of the directory tree on the right side, then open public. Download the noise recording your instructor placed in /public/module-04/ to your working folder. Open the recording in Audition with File → Open. It opens in the Waveform Editor. Find a short stretch of just the background noise, with no sound you want to keep, and select one to three seconds of it. Open Effects → Noise Reduction/Restoration → Noise Reduction (Process) and click Capture Noise Print. Audition now holds a noise print of the background. Select the whole file with Cmd-A, set the Noise Reduction amount to around 70 to 80 percent, press Preview to hear it, then Apply.

Keep a light touch. Pushed past about 90 percent, the reduction leaves the sound hollow and underwater. A little background left in sounds more natural than a heavily scrubbed file. Capturing a noise print and then reducing is the same two-step shape as the noise profile and Noise Reduction in your Audacity sample-prep pipeline.

one thing that's new to you

Open a file in the Waveform Editor and switch to the Spectral Frequency Display using the view button at the top of the editor. Instead of the loudness shape you know from a waveform, you see frequency from low to high up the side and time across the bottom, with louder sounds drawn brighter. A cough, a chair squeak, or a phone buzz shows up as its own bright shape.

Select that shape with the Marquee or Lasso tool and press Delete, or choose Favorites → Auto Heal to fill it with the sound around it (Auto Heal works on selections of four seconds or less).

build a small multitrack mix

Switch to the Multitrack Editor (press 0). Drag a few of your sounds from the Files panel onto separate tracks. Each track has a header on its left side, with a volume knob, a pan knob, a Mute button (M), and a Solo button (S). Set rough levels with the volume knobs and place sounds across the stereo field with pan. The controls match the Ableton mixer and the Toft console from Module 3: the volume knob sets the track's level like a channel fader, pan is pan, and solo lets you hear one track at a time. Audition's Mixer panel shows the same tracks as channel strips with faders.

Add one effect you already know. Select a track, open the Effects Rack, and add a Parametric Equalizer, the same kind of EQ as EQ Eight in Ableton. The rack also has the compression and limiting you've met before. On the Multitrack side the effect is nondestructive: you can change or remove it at any time.

Time and pitch, the tape link again

While you're here, try stretching a clip. Choose Clip → Stretch → Enable Global Clip Stretching, then drag the small white triangles in a clip's top corners. Select the clip and look at the Stretch settings in the Properties panel. The Varispeed type changes pitch and duration together: faster means higher and shorter, like speeding up tape. That's the coupling you met in Module 2 and saw in Audacity's Change Speed. The other types hold pitch while they stretch, the way Ableton's warp does.

export the mix

The session file isn't your finished audio. To get a sound file you can hand off, use File → Export → Multitrack Mixdown → Entire Session, choose WAV at 48 kHz, 32-bit, and save it into your working folder. It's the same project-versus-export split you've made in every module: the .sesx holds your editing decisions, and the exported WAV is the file other people can play.

end of session

what to upload today

  1. Save your session in Audition. Your .sesx and its audio are in the folder you chose inside ~/Documents/netid/.
  2. During the upload phase of the routine, select everything in ~/Documents/netid/ and drag it into your own folder on the server, choosing Overwrite if source newer when prompted. Today's practice session and your exported WAV go up with it.

Then continue with the rest of the card's end-of-session routine: disconnect and quit FileZilla, sign out of browser accounts, quit all apps, knobs back to zero, unplug everything, stow the gear back in the lab's gear storage, chair in.
