Sampling in Ableton

Turn your library into instruments, then sequence a short idea.

In Lab 1 your library sounds sat on the timeline as clips. Today they become instruments you play. You load a sample into Simpler and it plays across the keyboard, you build a small kit in a Drum Rack from a handful of sounds, and then you sequence a short part by drawing the notes into a clip. By the end you will have one short playable idea built from your own samples, saved and uploaded.

Instruments go on MIDI tracks

Everything today happens on MIDI tracks. An audio track holds recordings, the clips you worked with in Lab 1. A MIDI track holds an instrument and the notes that play it. Simpler and the Drum Rack are instruments, so they go on MIDI tracks. Dropping a sample straight onto an audio track plays it as a clip, the Lab 1 way; today you load it into an instrument instead.

Play one sound: Simpler

Simpler is an instrument that plays a single sample across the keyboard. Open Live's browser, find Simpler under Instruments, and drag it onto a MIDI track. Simpler opens with a Drop Sample Here box.

Drag one sound from your library onto that box. The waveform appears, and Simpler is now an instrument built from your sample. Play it: the sample plays at its recorded pitch around the middle of the keyboard, faster and higher as you go up, slower and lower as you go down. Simpler sets C3 as the root, the key that plays the sample unchanged, so the pitch you hear may sit higher or lower than you expect. The Transpose control in the Controls tab shifts the whole mapping if you want it centered differently.

To play it from the keyboard, click the track to select it, then click its round Arm button so the track listens for the keyboard. If you press keys and hear nothing, that arm is usually why.

What arming does

The round Arm button means something different on the two track types. On an audio track, arming preps the track to record. On a MIDI track, arming tells the track to listen, so the notes from your keyboard reach the instrument and you hear it. That connection is not automatic: arming is what makes it, and the keyboard plays the instrument while the track is armed. Once you sequence a part instead, the clip plays the instrument on its own, so arming is only for playing live like this. A few notes is all you need today, and you do not have to read music to place them.

Simpler can play your sample in three ways, chosen with the Mode switch on the left of the Sample tab. Two of them matter today.

Classic is the default, and it plays your sample like an instrument: the note sounds for as long as you hold the key, and it moves up and down in pitch as you play across the keyboard. Reach for it with melodic sounds, a sung note or a sustained tone you want to play as a line. Classic gives you the full ADSR envelope you met in Module 2, with its controls in the amp area of the Sample tab and a graph of the shape on the Controls tab. This is the first time you bend it on your own sound rather than a fixed tone. Try the extremes the way you did with the Envelope Explorer: a long attack and release to make your sample swell in and fade away like a pad, all four stages short to turn the same sample into a sharp hit. Move one control at a time and listen.

One-Shot is built for hits. It plays the whole sample from start to finish every time you press a key, however short the press, so reach for it with drums, claps, and single stabs. In place of the four-stage envelope it gives you two controls, Fade In and Fade Out. Fade In sets how long the sound takes to reach full volume at its start, and Fade Out sets how long it takes to fall away at its end, each up to two seconds, shown on the sample display. Leave both at zero and the sample fires at full and runs to its end. A short Fade In softens a hard attack, and a short Fade Out smooths a clipped tail or clears a click at the end. For a clean drum or stab, those two fades are usually all the shaping you need.

Build a kit: Drum Rack

A Drum Rack is a grid of pads, each pad holding one sample, each triggered by a key. Add a second MIDI track, then drag a Drum Rack from the browser onto it. You get a grid of empty pads.

Drag a sound from your library onto a pad. The pad fills, and Live loads the sample into a Simpler under the hood, ready to play. Drop a few more sounds onto other pads to build a small kit: something low, something sharp, something in between. Three or four pads is plenty.

Each pad answers to one key. The bottom-left pad of the visible grid is C1, and the pads climb from there. Play those keys, or click the pads with the mouse, to hear your kit. To rename a pad so you remember which sound is where, select it and press Cmd-R. The full reference is here:

Sequence a part

Recording vs sequencing

Recording MIDI means arming a track and playing a part on the keyboard, with Live capturing your timing and how hard you hit. Sequencing means drawing the notes into the grid by hand, placing each one exactly where you want it. Today you sequence. You draw the notes, so the keyboard is for hearing your instrument, and a steady hand on the mouse matters more than playing skill.

Make a MIDI clip on one of your instrument tracks: double-click an empty spot on the track in the Arrangement timeline to create an empty clip, then double-click the clip to open the MIDI Note Editor, the grid also called the piano roll. Each row is a pitch on Simpler, or a pad on the Drum Rack. Left to right is time.

Turn on Draw Mode (the pencil, or press B) and click in the grid to place notes. Place a few, press play, and listen. The strip below the notes is velocity, how hard each note plays; drag a note's velocity bar to make it softer or louder. These videos walk the whole process:

Build a short playable idea

Build one short playable idea, about fifteen to thirty seconds. It can be a melodic line from Simpler, a beat from your Drum Rack, or the two together. Keep it small. The point of today is that the sounds are your own and you are playing them, not just placing them on a timeline. Save as you go with Cmd-S.

Export

Export your idea to a single audio file at 48 kHz, 32-bit, saved into your working folder. Name it lastname-wk11-idea.wav. This is the same export you did in Lab 1.

End of session

What to upload today

  1. In Live, run File → Collect All and Save. This copies the library sounds you loaded into Simpler and the Drum Rack into the Set's Project folder, so the project travels as one piece and nothing goes missing.
  2. During the NAS-upload phase of the routine, upload your lastname/ working folder to [path]/students/lastname/, choosing Replace when prompted. The lastname-wk11 Project folder and your exported WAV go up with it.
  3. Verify the lastname-wk11 Project folder and lastname-wk11-idea.wav are on the NAS.

Then continue with the rest of the card's end-of-session routine: eject the NAS, sign out of browser accounts, quit all apps, knobs back to zero, unplug everything, stow the gear back in the lab's gear storage, chair in.
