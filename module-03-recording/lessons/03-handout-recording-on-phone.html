Lab reference · Phone recording

recording on your phone

How to capture sounds on your phone in a format we can use later.

You can add to your library outside the lab too. With the right app on your phone and a few settings set once, you can record anything that catches your ear: a kettle boiling, a bike chain ticking, footsteps in a stairwell, a friend's voice, a door that sticks. Wed Wk 7's lab moves these recordings into Audacity.

1 · the app for your phone

The course recommends two apps, one for each platform. Both are field-recording apps that export WAV files Audacity opens directly, and both give you control over how the recording is made, with no automatic "make it sound good" processing.

iPhone

Lossless Field Recorder by edson engineering. Free on the App Store: apps.apple.com/us/app/lossless-field-recorder/id1644208446. Requires iOS 15 or later.

Android

Field Recorder by Pfitzinger Voice Design. $5.95 on Google Play: play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=de.pfitzinger.rec. Its input settings let you switch off the phone maker's automatic processing.

Install whichever one matches your phone, accept the microphone permission when the app asks, and move to setup.

LocalSend, for moving recordings to the lab Macs

Install one more free app on your phone: LocalSend, from the App Store (iPhone) or Google Play (Android). It sends files from your phone to a computer on the same Wi-Fi network, and it's already installed on the lab Macs. Open it once and allow the permissions it asks for. The phone recordings into Audacity lab on Wed Wk 7 walks through sending a recording with it.

2 · setup, by platform

iPhone (Lossless Field Recorder)

The app has no separate Settings screen. Tap the microphone icon on the main screen to open the three choices that shape every recording: mic mode, frequency mode, and pickup pattern.

Mic mode picks which of the iPhone's three built-in microphones to record from. Modern iPhones have a bottom mic (next to the charging port), a front mic (in the notch or Dynamic Island at the top of the screen), and a back mic (next to the rear cameras). Each one faces a different direction:

  • Bottom is the iPhone's main mic, the same one used for phone calls. Start here when you're not sure.
  • Front faces the same direction as the screen and picks up whatever's in front of you when you're looking at the phone. Use it for sounds you're examining up close, or when the phone is lying screen-up.
  • Back faces the same direction as the rear cameras. Use it when you can point the camera at the source: a faucet across the kitchen, a door opening, a person on the other side of a table. For surface sounds, lay the phone face-up on the surface, and the back mic points straight into it.

Frequency mode sets the sample rate. Choose 48 kHz. (A 96 kHz recording makes files twice as large, with no audible improvement from a phone mic.) The app records at 24-bit depth automatically, and every recording you make is a 48 kHz, 24-bit WAV file, the format your Audacity projects use. The recordings go into the library with nothing to convert.

Pickup pattern is the app's name for the polar pattern: how the mic responds to direction. The options depend on which mic mode you picked:

  • With bottom, only omni is available. The bottom mic is a single physical microphone with no directional shaping.
  • With front, you get omni, cardioid, or stereo. Omni picks up from all directions equally. Cardioid picks up what's in front of the mic and rejects sound from behind. Stereo combines multiple iPhone mics into a left/right image.
  • With back, you get omni, subcardioid, or stereo. Subcardioid is like cardioid but less directional, and it picks up some sound from the sides.
polar patterns

Monday's reading introduced cardioid as one polar pattern out of several. The image below shows six of them. Each shape is the mic's sensitivity to sound from each direction: the front of the mic is at the top of each shape (the Shure chart in Monday's reading puts the front at the bottom), and the further the shape extends in a direction, the more strongly the mic picks up sound from there.

The iPhone app's pickup-pattern names match these shapes: omni is the sphere, cardioid is the heart, and subcardioid (not pictured) is between the two. Stereo isn't on this chart.

For most sounds, start with omni. Try cardioid or subcardioid when you want to reject room noise or other sounds coming from behind the mic. Leave stereo off: the samples in your library are mono WAV files (Project 2).

Android (Field Recorder)

Two things to set:

Set the input configuration first. The switch labeled In:MicA at the top of the display cycles through several input configurations: In:MicA through In:MicE, plus In:Raw. Each one picks a microphone and how Android pre-processes the signal before the app gets it. Most configurations apply manufacturer-chosen processing (automatic gain control, noise suppression, echo cancellation). In:Raw turns that pre-processing off, and it's the setting you want.

On a few phones, In:Raw doesn't work. Start by checking the Factory menu (tap "Factory" in the display) for a preset uploaded by someone with your phone model: cloud presets are listed by manufacturer and model, and a matching one already has the right input configuration. If there's no matching preset, set the input to In:Raw and test it: tap the pause button to enter record-ready mode, then tap near the phone and watch the level meters. If the meters move, you're set. If they don't, cycle through In:MicA to In:MicE until you find one that works.

Then set the sample rate and bit depth. Open Settings → Rec. Set sample rate to 48 kHz, the rate your Audacity projects use. Turn on 24 bit file write if your phone supports it (it needs Android 12 or later). You then get 48 kHz, 24-bit WAV files, the same format the iPhone app produces.

Once you have a configuration that works, save it as a preset: tap the preset name in the display, then Save, and give it a name you'll recognize. Field Recorder loads your last-used preset on every restart, and you only set this up once.

3 · the take

airplane mode on

A text message or phone call mid-take ruins the recording, and on some phones the cellular and Wi-Fi radios leak into the audio as a faint clicking or buzzing. Turn airplane mode on before you start and off when you're done.

set the phone down

Your hand on the phone produces handling noise louder than most of the sounds you'll want to record. Place the phone on a stable surface, prop it against something, or wedge it between two objects. If you have to hold it, hold the case as still as you can and keep your fingers away from the mic openings.

distance

Close (a few inches from the source) captures fine detail, and the source is louder than the room. Far (a few feet) captures the room around the source, with the source as one element inside it. Neither is right or wrong; they're different recordings of the same event. When you're not sure, take both and pick later.

watch the level meter

Both apps show a real-time level meter in record-ready mode and during recording. Watch the peak indicator. If it keeps hitting the top of the meter (or a red clip indicator lights up), the signal is too loud and the recording will distort: move the phone farther from the source. If the peaks stay low, move closer. Aim for the same target as in the lab, peaks between -12 and -6 dBFS. On a meter without numbers, aim for the upper third, clearly below the top.

wind

Phone mics have no wind protection. Any breeze gives you a low rumbling whoosh louder than everything else. One fix is to cup your hand loosely around the mic opening to break the airflow. Another is to record from a position that puts a wall, your body, or a piece of furniture between the wind and the phone.

4 · what's next

Keep recording anything that catches your ear, and leave the files in the app until the Wed Wk 7 lab.

Some things that record well on a phone, to get you started:

  • small mechanical sounds, such as zippers, latches, hinges, switches, keys, and locks
  • domestic sounds, such as a kettle, water running, ice in a glass, a fridge cycling, or a fan
  • object-on-object sounds, such as pebbles in a jar, a wooden spoon on a pot, or a coin on a table
  • body sounds, such as footsteps on different surfaces, a hand brushing fabric, or a clap in a stairwell
  • found environments, such as an elevator, a hallway, a courtyard at dusk, or a parking garage

Record more than you think you'll use.
